# Pronunciation & Listening

**Course basis:** Lessons 02, 03, 10, 11, 13, 14, 16, 18, and 19.

**Study purpose:** Recognise words in natural speech and pronounce them clearly without mechanically deleting sounds.

**Source distinction:** This is a consolidated review of the existing lesson notes, including their editorial corrections. Explanations and practice examples are edited or newly written study material, not verbatim classroom quotations. No original recordings were analysed for this compilation; an uncertain transcript is not proof of a pronunciation error.

## 1. Spelling Gives Clues, Not Guaranteed Pronunciations

**Lessons 03 and 16.** Letters and sounds are different. Two written vowels do not necessarily form a diphthong, and a consonant letter may belong to a group representing one sound.

| Spelling | Useful examples | Contrast to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ou / ow** | **loud, cow** often contain /aʊ/. | **soup /suːp/** and **snow /snəʊ/** have different vowels. |
| **oi / oy** | **coin, boy** contain /ɔɪ/. | Learn the sound with whole words. |
| **oo** | **moon /muːn/** | **book /bʊk/**; **blood /blʌd/**. |
| **ew / ue / ui** | **chew /tʃuː/**, **blue /bluː/**, **fruit /fruːt/** | **few /fjuː/** includes /j/; **build /bɪld/** has /ɪ/. |
| **ph** | **phone** usually has /f/. | Do not infer the pronunciation of every unfamiliar spelling from one word. |
| **gh** | **enough** has /f/. | **night** has no /ɡ/ or /f/; **ghost** begins with /ɡ/. |
| **tch** | **watch, butcher** contain /tʃ/. | This is one affricate sound, not a reason to say only /ʃ/. |

These IPA examples use common reference pronunciations; accents vary.

## 2. Silent Letters: The Ten Groups Reviewed

| Letter | Examples with no separate sound for that letter | Boundary of the pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **G** | **gnome, sign, design, campaign** | **Signal** and **magnet** retain /ɡ/. |
| **U** | **guitar, guess, biscuit** | **Quick** starts with /kw/; **GU/QU** does not always remove /w/. |
| **C** | **scene, muscle** have no /k/. | **School** contains /sk/; **conscious** has /ʃ/, not /s/. |
| **L** | **talk, walk, calm, half, would, could, should** | **Always** and **shoulder** have /l/. **Almond** has accepted variants. |
| **P** | **psychology, pneumonia, pterodactyl, raspberry, cupboard** | **Upbeat** is not governed by a universal “P before B is silent” rule. |
| **B** | **climb, thumb, crumb, debt, doubt, subtle** | **Member** and **obtain** retain /b/. |
| **T** | **listen, castle, whistle, wrestle** | **Often** can have /t/ or no /t/. **Left** and **gift** retain /t/ in dictionary forms. |
| **H** | **honest, hour** | **What/where** usually start with /w/, but some accents distinguish **wh**. **Who** begins with /h/. |
| **K** | **know, knife, knee** | The familiar initial **kn-** pattern does not apply to every K–N sequence. |
| **W** | **write, wrong, wrist, wrestle** | **Wrestle /ˈresəl/** also has no /t/. |

**Two particularly useful corrections:** Both pronunciations of **often**, with and without /t/, are accepted. **Almond** is not universally pronounced with a silent L or one fixed vowel.

## 3. /n/, /ŋ/, and /ŋɡ/

**Lesson 16.** The usual dictionary ending of **-ing** is **/ɪŋ/**.

- **sin /sɪn/**: the front of the tongue forms /n/.
- **sing /sɪŋ/**: the back of the tongue forms /ŋ/; no extra /ɡ/.
- **singing /ˈsɪŋɪŋ/**: two /ŋ/ sounds in this reference pronunciation.
- **singer**: normally no separate /ɡ/ in the reference forms studied.
- **finger**: contains /ŋɡ/ in those forms.

Informal **-in' /ɪn/** is a real regional and stylistic variation. It is not the standard dictionary explanation of every **-ing** word.

**Related correction:** The vowel in **cup** is commonly **/ʌ/**, not **/n/**. Compare **cup /kʌp/**, **put /pʊt/**, **cute /kjuːt/**, and **rule /ruːl/**; one simple U-spelling rule does not cover them all.

## 4. Six Different Features of Connected Speech

**Lessons 02, 10, 13, and 14.** Some earlier lesson explanations about final consonants were too absolute. Use these distinctions when reviewing them.

| Feature | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Linking** | Sounds connect across a word boundary. | **turn it off**, **like a** |
| **Weak form** | An unstressed word has a reduced pronunciation. | **can /kən/**, **to /tə/**, **and /ən/** |
| **Assimilation** | A sound changes under a neighbouring sound's influence. | /n/ may become [m] in **green park**. |
| **Elision** | A sound is omitted in a suitable context. | /t/ may be omitted in **best man**. |
| **Unreleased stop** | The closure remains without a clear release burst. | Final /t/ before another consonant may behave this way. |
| **Flapping** | A brief tongue-tip contact [ɾ] occurs in suitable environments. | Common American pronunciations of **water** and **better**. |

**A weakly heard consonant has not necessarily disappeared.** Final /t/ and /d/ are not automatically deleted before every consonant. A flap is not simply the ordinary English /r/, and song pronunciation is not a universal model for conversation.

**Useful assimilation examples:** **ten bucks** may have [m] before /b/; **ten cars** may have [ŋ] before /k/. In **did you**, /d/ + /j/ can combine as [dʒ]. These are possible realisations, not compulsory changes.

## 5. Endings, Stress, and Accent

**Plural endings — Lessons 13, 14, and 18:**

- **/s/** after a voiceless non-sibilant: **books**.
- **/z/** after a voiced non-sibilant: **dogs, apples, kilometres**.
- **/ɪz/**, with accent variation, after a sibilant: **buses, watches**.

Make the ending clear without giving every final **s** strong stress.

**Word stress — Lesson 19:** **various** stresses the first syllable; **diversity** stresses the second. **Shop** and **show** differ in both vowel and final consonant in the reference accents studied.

**Can/can't — Lesson 02:** Listen to the sentence meaning, stress, vowel, and final consonant together. Do not rely only on vowel length or an audible /t/ release. Ask **“Did you say you can or you can't?”** when the distinction matters.

**Accent — Lessons 10 and 11:** Many American accents pronounce post-vocalic /r/ in **car**; a non-rhotic reference accent does not pronounce it there but may use linking /r/ in **car engine**. British and American accents each vary. Mixed features are not automatically errors; aim for intelligibility. A Russian trilled /r/ is a different consonant realisation, not a vowel.

## 6. Homophones: Use Context to Identify the Word

**Lesson 03.** Homophones share a pronunciation in a particular accent but have different meanings and often different spellings.

| Pair or group | Meanings to distinguish |
| --- | --- |
| **male / mail** | A sex category; letters or post. |
| **sea / see** | A body of salt water; perceive visually. |
| **pair / pear** | Two matching or associated things; a fruit. |
| **right / write** | Correct or a direction; produce written language. |
| **here / hear** | In this place; perceive sound. |
| **by / buy / bye** | A preposition; purchase; goodbye. |
| **flower / flour** | Part of a plant; an ingredient for baking. |
| **meat / meet** | Food from animals; encounter or come together. |
| **son / sun** | A male child; the star that gives Earth light. |
| **one / won** | The number; the past of win. |
| **weight / wait** | How heavy something is; remain until something happens. |

**Eight/ate** are homophones when **ate** is /eɪt/; /et/ also occurs. **Aunt/ant** are homophones in accents using /ænt/ for both, but not in accents that distinguish **aunt /ɑːnt/** from **ant /ænt/**.

**Practice sentence:** **I'll buy a pair of shoes by Friday.** The surrounding grammar and meaning identify the words, even when their sounds overlap with other words.

## 7. A Short Listening Routine

1. Listen to one short segment without text and write what you hear.
2. Check the wording against a reliable transcript.
3. Identify one feature: stress, linking, weak form, assimilation, or elision.
4. Say the phrase clearly, then imitate its rhythm at a comfortable speed.
5. Listen again without the text.

Do not create pronunciation rules from uncertain song lyrics or automatic transcription alone.

## 8. Review Practice

1. Does **watch** end in /ʃ/ or /tʃ/?
2. Is /t/ in **often** necessarily wrong?
3. Which has a separate /ɡ/ in the reference forms: **singer** or **finger**?
4. Give the ending sounds in **books, apples, watches**.
5. If /n/ becomes [m] in **ten bucks**, is that assimilation or deletion?
6. Explain why an inaudible release does not prove that a final stop disappeared.

**Answers:** 1. **/tʃ/**. 2. **No; both forms are accepted.** 3. **Finger**. 4. **/s/, /z/, /ɪz/**. 5. **Assimilation**. 6. **The closure can remain without a distinct release burst.**

**Read aloud:** **I know the singer. / Please write the correct sign. / I often listen while walking. / Did you buy three apples?** Record one careful version and one comfortable conversational version.
