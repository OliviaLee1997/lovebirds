### Added model answer

I like having time to walk slowly and pay attention to what's around me. It's a simple way to switch off after a busy week.

**Length note:** This is a short model paragraph. For a full Part 2 practice answer, add a specific visit and a personal detail you can support.

### It Is Easy for Me to Get There

| Intended meaning | Natural expression |
| --- | --- |
| Reaching the park is easy. | **It's easy to get there.** |
| Reaching it is easy for me personally. | **It's easy for me to get there.** |
| I can reach it without difficulty. | **I can get there easily.** |
| The park is accessible. | **The park is easy to reach.** |

**“I'm easy to get there”** does not express the intended relationship. In **“It's easy for me to get there,”** **it** introduces an evaluation of the action.

However, **a person can be the subject of some easy-to structures**: **“I'm easy to contact”** means other people can contact me easily. The problem is not that **I am easy to…** is always forbidden; the sentence must give the intended meaning.

The summary's **“I am easy to make mistakes”** is described as a **tutor's example**, not necessarily the learner's own wording. Natural alternatives are **“I tend to make mistakes”** or **“It's easy for me to make mistakes when I'm tired.”**

### Expressing Relaxation Naturally

- **take a break from work / take a break from my usual routine**
- **switch off after work / unwind after a busy day**
- **get some relief from work-related stress**
- **get away from the noise / enjoy some peace and quiet**

**Relief from work** may be understandable, but **a break from work** or **relief from work-related stress** often states the intended meaning more precisely.

## 2. Plurals: Meaning and Pronunciation

### People and Peoples

- For ordinary counting, use **one person → two people → many people**.
- **Peoples** is a real word meaning distinct populations or ethnic groups, as in **Indigenous peoples**. It is not the usual plural for several individuals visiting a park.

### Pronounce the Ending Without Overstressing It

| Ending | Example | Pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **/s/** | **books** | After a voiceless non-sibilant sound, such as /k/. |
| **/z/** | **apples, kilometres/kilometers** | After a voiced non-sibilant sound. |
| **/ɪz/** | **buses, watches** | After a sibilant sound, such as /s/ or /tʃ/. |

The goal is an audible, natural ending, not strong stress on every plural **s**.

**Distance:** **It's three kilometres away.**  
**Compound modifier:** **It's a three-kilometre walk.** Here **kilometre** stays singular inside the modifier.

## 3. An Unexpected Event: A Team Competition

### Reported content

- The learner described taking part in a **student team competition** in another region some years earlier.
- The summary lists **building the car, circuit design, and presenting the design** as three parts. Their precise order and the official competition format are not established by the supplied material.
- During the summer holiday, a teacher unexpectedly called and asked her to return to the university for the second stage. She had already bought a ticket to go home.
- Tickets were difficult to obtain. She and her teammates took an overnight train without seats and reportedly got no sleep.
- Despite feeling exhausted and in a poor mood, the team won an award for the project.
- The project outcome gave the team a sense of achievement and encouraged further learning.

### Added cue card and structure

Describe something unexpected that happened to you. Say **when it happened**, **what you had planned**, **what changed**, and explain **the result and your reaction**.

**Structure:** Original plan → Unexpected call → Travel difficulty → Competition result → Reflection.

### Added model answer

> An unexpected experience I remember happened when I was at university, some years ago. I was taking part in a student team competition in another region with a team of other students. The work involved building a car, designing its circuits and explaining our design.
>
> During the summer holiday, I had already bought a ticket to go home when a teacher called and asked me to return to the university for the next stage. That meant changing my plans at very short notice.
>
> Getting back was difficult because train tickets were scarce. My teammates and I ended up travelling overnight without seats, and we didn't get any sleep. By the time we arrived, we were exhausted and not in a particularly good mood.
>
> Despite that, our team went on to win an award for the project. The result also encouraged me to keep developing my skills, so it mattered beyond the competition itself.
>
> Looking back, what stands out is how differently the experience ended from how it began. I had been disappointed about changing my holiday plans, but the unexpected return led to something valuable. It reminded me that a difficult start doesn't necessarily determine the outcome.

**Model note:** The story is generalised for practice; connecting language and reflection are added study material.

### Tense and Voice

- **It happened**, not **it was happened**. **Happen** does not take a passive form in this meaning.
- **The event happened suddenly** uses an adverb to describe the action.
- **The event was unexpected** uses an adjective to describe the event. **“The event was sudden”** can also be grammatical, but suddenness and unexpectedness are not identical.
- **I had already bought a ticket when the teacher called** clearly places the purchase before the call.
- **We didn't have any seats**, not **we don't have any seats**, when narrating that finished journey.
- **By the time we arrived, we hadn't slept** relates the lack of sleep to the later arrival.

### No Choice: Both Forms Are Correct

> **We didn't have any choice. / We had no choice.**

Both are grammatical. The second is shorter; it is not a mandatory correction of the first. In a past narrative, **“We don't have any choice”** would normally need to become past tense unless you are quoting what someone said at the time.

Useful complete pattern: **We had no choice but to take the overnight train.**

## 4. Vocabulary: Choose the Meaning Before Replacing the Word

| Expression | Appropriate use | Important distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **competition / contest** | An organised event in which people compete. | Either may fit this story. **The event** is another useful reference after it has been introduced. |
| **rivalry** | An ongoing competitive relationship between people or groups. | **A rivalry between two teams** is natural. **We entered a rivalry** does not mean **we entered a competition**. |
| **stage / phase** | A part of a process or event. | **Phase** suits a process such as design or testing. **Round** may fit a formal contest round, but do not invent a format absent from the source. |
| **award / prize** | Recognition or a prize received for an achievement. | **We won an award for the project** fits the reported account. |
| **reward** | A benefit or payment for an action, effort, or service. | It is a valid word, but it is not usually the most precise name for a formal competition award. |
| **go back / return** | Travel to a previous place. | **We went back to the university. / We returned to the university.** |

**Back can be a verb**, as in **“I back your proposal”**, meaning support it. In **“back to the university,”** however, it does not by itself express the required action of returning; use **went back**, **got back**, or **returned**.

**Keep the destination:** **Returned home** and **returned to the university** are not interchangeable. Correcting the grammar should not change where the person went. Use **went home / returned home**, without **to**, but **went to the university**.

**Expectations:**

- **We had no expectations.** — a broad statement.
- **We had no expectation of winning.** — grammatical singular use with a specific expectation.
- **We didn't expect to win.** — often the clearest spoken version.

Do not replace words solely to avoid repetition. Repeating **competition** is preferable to choosing a word that changes the meaning.

## 5. Reading Habits and Discussion Topics

### A. A Book About Politics and National Development

**Practice reading topic:** A book about social and economic development can be discussed through its themes, arguments, evidence and the questions it raises. No personal political position is attributed to a participant.

**Added model paragraph:**

> I'm currently reading a book about politics and national development. It discusses why socialism is considered suitable for China and how countries organise their resources and development. I'm particularly interested in the role of technology and the differences between managing a very large country and a smaller one. I'd like to understand how decisions for the project affect different regions.

**Attribution:** **“The book argues that…”** or **“It discusses why…”** helps distinguish a book's position from an established fact or a claim you are personally defending. Do not invent the book's title or specific conclusions.

### B. Children's Reading and Fairy Tales

**Reported views:** the learner preferred age-appropriate stories connected to real life and questioned the usefulness of fairy tales. The tutor reportedly acknowledged imagination as a benefit and discussed reading aloud to children who cannot yet read independently.

These are discussion positions. **“Fairy tales are useless”** is an absolute judgement, not a fact established by the lesson.

**Added ways to express the preference accurately:**

- **I tend to prefer realistic stories for children because they can introduce situations children may actually encounter.**
- **I'm less convinced by stories that present an unrealistic picture of life.**
- **The suitability of a story depends on the child's age and how adults discuss it with them.**

**Added alternative perspective for debate:** Imaginative stories can also provide material for discussing emotions, choices, and consequences. You can acknowledge this possibility without changing your own preference:

> **I personally prefer realistic stories, although I can see why some parents value fairy tales for their imagination and storytelling.**

Reading aloud does not require children to be able to read independently. Avoid treating **being read to** and **reading alone** as the same activity.

### C. Paper Books and E-books

| Format | Reported benefits | Qualifications to keep the discussion precise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Paper books** | the learner finds them more comfortable for her eyes, easy to annotate, and helpful for sustained concentration. | Eye comfort varies with lighting, print size, reading duration, and the person. Present this as a preference, not a universal medical advantage. |
| **E-books** | Convenient, easy to search, and sometimes cheaper. | Price varies. Many digital readers also allow notes, and a dedicated e-reader or a device with notifications disabled may be less distracting than a phone. |

**Added model answer:**

> I usually prefer paper books when I want to read for a long time. I find them more comfortable, and I like being able to write notes in the margins. I also find it easier to concentrate when there are no notifications to check. E-books have advantages too, especially for searching or carrying several books at once. For me, the better choice depends on whether I want to study something closely or access information quickly.

### D. Libraries as Reading and Social Spaces

**Reported observation:** the learner felt that some local libraries were being used for socialising, dating, or recreation, making it harder for her to concentrate. She preferred borrowing books and reading at home.

**Added expression:**

> **Some libraries I've visited feel more like social spaces than quiet reading rooms. I can see the value of community activities, but I prefer a separate quiet area when I need to concentrate. If that isn't available, I'd rather borrow a book and read at home.**

Keep the scope at **some libraries I've visited** unless you have evidence for a broader claim. Community activities and quiet study can both be legitimate library functions.

## 6. Personal Language Review

| Reported wording or issue | Correction or clarification |
| --- | --- |
| **peoples**, meaning several individuals | **people**. **Peoples** has a different use for distinct populations. |
| **three kilometer**, giving a distance | **three kilometres / three kilometers**; pronounce the final /z/. |
| **I'm easy to get there** | **It's easy for me to get there. / I can get there easily.** |
| **I first visit…**, referring to [year] | **I first visited…** |
| **it was happened** | **it happened** |
| **we don't have a seat**, in the train story | **We didn't have any seats. / None of us had a seat.** |
| **we didn't have any choice** | Already grammatical. **We had no choice** is a concise alternative. |
| **back to the university**, intended as a full past clause | **We went back to the university.** |
| **no expectation** | Not automatically wrong: **We had no expectation of winning** is grammatical. |
| **a national reward**, referring to competition recognition | **an award for the project / a competition prize**, as appropriate to the actual result. |
| **The event was sudden happen** | **The event happened suddenly. / It happened unexpectedly.** Choose the intended meaning. |
| **I wish you have a good time** | **I hope you have a good time. / Have a good time!** **Wish you + noun phrase** is another pattern, as in **I wish you a pleasant trip.** |

The last correction concerns **verb patterns**, not merely an unnecessary **have**. For an ordinary positive wish about someone's forthcoming experience, **hope + clause** is natural.
