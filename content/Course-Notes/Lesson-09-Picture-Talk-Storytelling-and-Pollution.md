## 1. The Core Skill of This Lesson: Structuring a Long Monologue

**Reported feedback:** In the one-minute topic statement, the tutor advised **organising thoughts first, grouping viewpoints, and then speaking in order** so as not to get stuck halfway. Two further points were emphasised: use **simple vocabulary to express complex ideas**, and keep the **tense consistent**, especially past tense when narrating past events.

**Added study framework — the 30-second plan:** Before you speak, spend a few seconds choosing three groups. Say the group name, then the details inside it.

| Step | What you do | Signpost language |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Frame** | Say what you will cover. | “I'd like to talk about three things: …” |
| **2. Group** | Give each group a label. | “First… / Second… / Finally…” |
| **3. Support** | Add one detail or example per group. | “For example… / In my case…” |
| **4. Land** | End with one opinion or summary. | “So overall, I think…” |

**Why simple words win:** When you search for an advanced word, your brain is doing two jobs at once — choosing vocabulary and building grammar. That is what produces mid-sentence stalls. A simple sentence delivered steadily sounds more fluent than a complex one delivered in fragments.

**Stalling rescue phrases — added study material:**

> Let me put it another way.  
> What I mean is…  
> That's not quite the word — basically, …  
> Anyway, the main point is…

**Clarification:** These phrases are not filler to be memorised and overused. Their function is to keep your turn going while you recover, instead of stopping in silence.

## 2. Picture One: The Great Wall (Cultural Symbol)

### Reported Scene and Discussion

- The weather was described as **sunny and comfortable**. Winter was inferred because people were wearing **jackets** and the grass had **turned yellow**.
- Most tourists were described as Chinese, with some **taking selfies**.
- The Great Wall was presented as **a symbol of Chinese culture**, historically used for **defence against enemies**, with the Mongols given as an example.
- **Beijing is in northern China** is a useful geographical description.
- The summary records a related remark about buying snacks; the reported wording was “We just bought some snacks from supermarket.”

**Description clarification:** The season is an **inference**, not a fact. Yellow grass and jackets suggest late autumn or winter, but they can also appear in a dry northern spring. In speaking, mark the difference with **seems to**, **looks like**, or **I'd guess** rather than stating it as certain. On the history: the walls were built and rebuilt over many centuries by different dynasties, primarily for defence and border control — a single enemy such as the Mongols is an example, not the whole story.

### Useful Language

- **a symbol of Chinese culture** — uncountable here; not “Chinese cultures”.
- **be built for military purposes / as a defence** — the natural collocation for a fortification.
- **It dates back to…** / **It was first built over 2,000 years ago.**
- **take a selfie / take photos as a souvenir**
- **in the north of China / in northern China** — both are correct; **the north of** needs the article.
- **about [distance] away from…**

**Edited model answer — added study material:**

> This picture shows tourists visiting the Great Wall on a sunny day. It looks like late autumn or winter, because people are wearing jackets and the grass has turned yellow. Most of the visitors are Chinese, and some are taking selfies as a souvenir. The Great Wall is a symbol of Chinese culture. It was built for military purposes, mainly to defend against enemies from the north. Beijing is in the north of China.

## 3. Picture Two: Dining Together (Lifestyle)

### Reported Scene and Discussion

- A man and a woman were described as eating **vegetable burgers** and talking.
- They were interpreted as **friends** rather than a couple, because neither was wearing a ring and no obvious couple characteristics were visible.
- The learner said she prefers **eating out** to cooking.
- She recently ate **Moroccan-style chicken pizza**, which she described as delicious and **not spicy**.

**Interpretation clarification:** A ring is weak evidence. People may be unmarried, may not wear jewellery, or may simply be colleagues. In an exam or a conversation, present this as a guess: **They might be friends** rather than **They are friends**.

### Useful Language

| Expression | Added example |
| --- | --- |
| **a veggie burger / a vegetarian burger** | More natural than “vegetable burger”. |
| **eat out / eat in** | I usually eat out at weekends. |
| **cook for myself** | I rarely cook for myself on weekdays. |
| **It's delicious / It's not spicy at all** | The pizza was delicious and not spicy at all. |
| **It's too spicy for me** | Sichuan food is often too spicy for me. |
| **order / share a table / split the bill** | We shared a table and split the bill. |

**Tense note:** Describe a specific meal you already ate in the **past simple** — “The pizza **was** delicious.” Use the **present simple** for your general preference — “I **prefer** eating out.”

**Edited model answer — added study material:**

> In this picture, a man and a woman are eating veggie burgers and talking. They might be friends rather than a couple, because neither of them is wearing a ring — though of course that's only a guess. Personally, I prefer eating out to cooking. Recently I tried a Moroccan-style chicken pizza. It was delicious and not spicy at all.

## 4. Picture Three: Runners (Sports Topic)

### Reported Scene and Discussion

- The image was described as showing **four adults — three women and one man — running**, with a **blurred background**.
- The learner judged that the image was **AI-generated**.
- She suggested the runners come from **different countries**, possibly including the US, and that their expressions look **happy**.
- She said she enjoys **jogging** and considers it **friendly for beginners**.
- She prefers to use **spare time after lunch** for exercise.

**Vocabulary clarification:** **Jogging** is slow, steady running for fitness; **running** is the general activity; **sprinting** is short and fast. “Friendly for beginners” is understandable, but the natural forms are **beginner-friendly** or **suitable for beginners**. Motion blur is a real photographic effect, so a blurred background alone does not prove an image is AI-generated — say **It looks AI-generated to me** rather than asserting it.

### Useful Language

- **a blurred background / out of focus**
- **beginner-friendly / suitable for beginners**
- **go jogging / go for a jog**
- **in my spare time / in my free time**
- **after lunch / during my lunch break**
- **build up stamina** — improve your ability to keep exercising.
- **It's easy on the knees.** — gentler than high-impact exercise.

**Edited model answer — added study material:**

> This picture shows four adults running — three women and one man. The background is blurred, so they might have been moving fast. To be honest, it looks AI-generated to me. They all look happy, and they might come from different countries. I enjoy jogging myself, because it's beginner-friendly and doesn't need any special equipment. I usually go jogging in my spare time after lunch.

## 5. Personal Narrative: A High-School Story

### Reported Story and Feedback

- In high school, the learner's **maths teacher** advised her to **transfer to another school** because her maths grades were poor.
- Later, encouraged by **classmates at the same school**, she **regained confidence** and eventually entered a **university**.
- The tutor affirmed her **resilience**, and pointed out that **advice should be filtered**: one negative evaluation is not a good reason to change your environment; it is better to work on your own ability.
- The learner's reported wording included “My math teacher doesn't like me”, “She suggested me to go to another high school”, and “Don't stuck in one tough situation”.

**Added study point — this story is your best tense practice material.** A narrative about high school lives in the **past simple**. The most common slip is starting in the past and drifting into the present halfway through. Set the time frame in your first sentence, then hold it.

| Time frame | Tense | Added example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Set the scene | **past simple / past continuous** | When I **was** in high school, my maths **wasn't** strong. |
| Sequence of events | **past simple** | My teacher **suggested** that I **move** to another school. |
| Earlier than another past event | **past perfect** | By then, I **had already lost** confidence. |
| Result that continues now | **present perfect / present simple** | Since then, I **have learned** to filter advice. |

### Useful Language

- **regain confidence / rebuild confidence**
- **take someone's advice / filter advice / take it with a pinch of salt**
- **a negative evaluation / harsh criticism**
- **be resilient / show resilience / bounce back**
- **get into a university** — “get into” is the natural verb for admission.
- **Don't get stuck in a tough situation.** — **stuck** is an adjective, so it needs **be** or **get**.

**Edited model answer — added study material:**

> When I was in high school, my maths grades were poor, and my maths teacher suggested that I move to another school. That hurt, and for a while I didn't believe in myself. Later, some classmates at my school encouraged me, and I gradually regained my confidence. In the end, I got into a university. Looking back, I think the most important lesson is that we should filter advice. One negative evaluation isn't a good reason to change your whole environment. It's better to work on your ability and not get stuck in one tough situation.

## 6. Environmental Discussion: Pollution in China

### Reported Discussion

- The learner's **hometown** once suffered **severe air pollution** because the local economy depended on **coal**; the situation has improved with the spread of **electric vehicles** and other environmental improvements.
- **Fuel vehicles still dominate in the north** because of cold winters, while **electric vehicles are more popular in the south**.
- **Plastic waste** was described as causing **land pollution**, and scientists were said to have discovered **insects that can decompose plastic**.
- **Ocean pollution** was attributed mainly to **tourists littering** and **ship emissions**.
- Current measures mentioned: **cleaners clearing ocean trash** and **public awareness raised through promotional activities**.
- Japan's discharge of nuclear wastewater into the ocean was described by the learner as **a disastrous act**.
- Her suggestions for further improvement: **reduce vehicle use, promote electric transport, develop new technologies**.

**Vocabulary clarification:**

- **Fuel vehicles** is a direct translation. Natural English uses **petrol cars** (British), **gasoline cars** (American), **fuel-powered cars**, or **conventional cars**. The counterpart is **electric vehicles (EVs)** or **electric cars**.
- **Land pollution** is understood, but **soil pollution** or **land contamination** is more precise for plastic waste in the ground. **Litter** is the everyday word for rubbish left in public places.
- **Promotional activities** usually means marketing. For the environment, say **public awareness campaigns** or **education campaigns**.
- Cold weather really does reduce EV battery range and charging speed, so the north–south difference the learner described is a reasonable observation rather than a fixed rule.

### Fact-check note 1: insects that eat plastic — added study material

The reported claim is broadly real but easily overstated. Researchers have shown that the **larvae** of certain insects — **waxworms** (*Galleria mellonella*), **mealworms** (*Tenebrio molitor*), and **superworms** (*Zophobas morio*) — can chew and partially biodegrade plastics such as polyethylene and polystyrene, largely with the help of **microbes in their guts**. This remains **laboratory-scale research**: the degradation is slow, and it is not currently a deployed method for cleaning up plastic waste.

**How to say it accurately in English:**

> Scientists have found that some insect larvae can break down certain plastics, but this is still early-stage research, not a solution we can use at scale yet.

*Sources: [Using Insect Larvae and Their Microbiota for Plastic Degradation (MDPI, Insects, 2025)](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4450/16/2/165); [Radical innovation breakthroughs of biodegradation of plastics by insects (Frontiers of Environmental Science & Engineering, 2024)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11783-024-1838-x).*

### Fact-check note 2: the ocean discharge — added study material

This is a **contested international issue**, and the words you choose carry a position. Facts to keep straight:

- Japan began discharging water from the Fukushima Daiichi plant into the Pacific in **August 2023**, and the discharge has continued in rounds since then; Chinese state media reported the **24th round** beginning in **October 2026**.
- The **IAEA** has stated that the discharge of ALPS-treated water **continues to meet international safety standards** and that the planned discharges would have a **negligible radiological impact**.
- **China and several other stakeholders strongly object**, arguing that the water contacted melted fuel and that long-term monitoring by all affected parties is necessary. Japanese fishing groups have also opposed the plan.
- Terminology differs by side: **“ALPS treated water”** (Japan, IAEA) versus **“nuclear-contaminated water”** (Chinese official usage). **“Nuclear sewage”** is common in everyday Chinese discussion but is not standard in international technical reporting.

**How to express a strong opinion with support — added study material:**

> In my view, releasing nuclear-contaminated water into the ocean is a serious mistake, because the ocean is shared by everyone and the long-term effects are hard to predict. I know the IAEA says the treated water meets international safety standards, but I'm not convinced that decades of monitoring can be guaranteed. That's why I think independent, long-term international monitoring matters.

**Language point:** Notice the structure — **opinion → reason → acknowledge the other side → restate your position**. This is far more persuasive in English than a bare adjective such as **disastrous**, and it protects you if your listener disagrees.

### Useful Language for the Whole Topic

| Category | Expressions |
| --- | --- |
| **Types** | air pollution, water pollution, soil pollution, plastic waste, ship emissions, littering |
| **Causes** | depend on coal, a coal-based economy, exhaust fumes, single-use plastics |
| **Change** | has improved, air quality has got better, emissions have dropped |
| **Measures** | raise public awareness, launch awareness campaigns, promote electric transport, sort rubbish, ban single-use plastics |
| **Hedging** | it seems to me, as far as I know, I'm not an expert, but… |

## 7. Argument Structure: Phenomenon → Cause → Solution

**Added study framework.** The supplied insight recommended practising this deeper argumentative structure. It is the same three-group plan from Section 1, applied to a topic.

**Model answer — added study material:**

> **Phenomenon.** When I was young, the air in my hometown was often badly polluted. You could smell it, and on some days you couldn't see the mountains.  
> **Cause.** The main reason was that the local economy depended on coal — for factories, for heating, and for cars.  
> **Solution.** Things have improved a lot in recent years, mainly because electric vehicles have become popular and old factories have been upgraded. In the north, petrol cars are still mainstream, because cold winters reduce battery range. In the south, EVs are much more common.  
> **My view.** To go further, I think we should use cars less, expand electric public transport, and keep investing in new technologies. Air quality is a good example of a problem that changes slowly but really can be fixed.

**Practise the same shape** on: plastic waste in your city · ocean litter near tourist areas · food delivery packaging · your own exercise habit.

## 8. Grammar and Expression Review

The first column adapts **reported wording from the supplied error analysis**, including its timestamps. It is not audio-verified. Revisions preserve the likely intended meaning.

### A. Articles and Prepositions

| Reported wording | Corrected or more natural version | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “We just bought some snacks from supermarket.” | **We just bought some snacks from the supermarket.** / **… at the supermarket.** | A singular countable noun needs an article: **the supermarket**. |
| “I live in north of China now.” | **I live in the north of China now.** / **I live in northern China.** | Compass regions take **the**: **the south of**, **the north of**. |
| “Beijing located in the north of China.” | **Beijing is located in the north of China.** | **Located** needs the verb **be**; or simply **Beijing is in northern China**. |
| “A insect can eat plastic.” | **An insect can eat plastic.** | **an** before a vowel sound. |
| “We have a environment problem.” | **We have an environmental problem.** | **an** before a vowel sound; the adjective form is **environmental**. |

### B. Tense and Verb Form

| Reported wording | Corrected or more natural version | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Its built for army purpose.” | **It was built for military purposes.** | **Its** = possessive; **It's** = it is. For a completed historical construction, **was built** is more natural than **is built**. Use **military purposes**, plural. |
| “Everything will goes.” | **Everything will go well.** / **Everything will be fine.** | After a modal (**will, can, should, must**) always use the **base verb** — never **-s**. |
| “My math teacher doesn't like me.” | **My maths teacher didn't like me.** | Narrating the past → **past simple**. Hold the same time frame through the whole story. |
| “It's hard to decided it.” | **It's hard to decide.** / **It's hard to tell.** | **to + base verb**. Also, **decide** usually takes **on**: **decide on a plan**. |
| “Don't stuck in one tough situation.” | **Don't get stuck in one tough situation.** | **Stuck** is an adjective, so it needs **be** or **get**. Negative imperative: **Don't get…**. |
| “Why was young? We have a environment problem…” | **When I was young, we had an environmental problem…** | Reconstructed as a time clause plus past simple. The transcript does not establish exactly what was said. |
| “She suggested me to go to another high school.” | **She suggested that I go to another high school.** / **She advised me to go to another high school.** | **Suggest** never takes **someone to do**. Use **suggest that + subject + base verb**, **suggest doing**, or switch to **advise someone to do**. |

### C. Number, Agreement, and Word Choice

| Reported wording | Corrected or more natural version | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “There are three girls and one boys.” | **There are three girls and one boy.** | **One** → singular noun. |
| “Chinese cultures.” | **Chinese culture.** | A country's culture as a whole is treated as uncountable or singular. **Cultures** is used when comparing several distinct cultures. |
| “The grass turns yellow.” | **The grass is yellow.** / **The grass has turned yellow.** | **Turns** suggests a repeated or general change. For the state you can see now, use **is**; for a completed change, **has turned**. |
| “Taking a memory.” | **Taking a photo as a souvenir.** / **Capturing a memory.** | **Take a memory** is not a natural collocation. You **take a photo**; you **capture** or **keep** a memory. |
| “Based on call, call.” | **Based on coal.** | Word-choice error from similar sound: **coal** /kəʊl/ vs **call** /kɔːl/. Context was the hometown economy. |
| “The glass turns yellow.” | **The grass is yellow.** | Slip of the tongue: **grass** vs **glass**. |

### D. Two Sound Pairs to Drill — added study material

| Pair | Sounds | Minimal practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **coal / call** | /kəʊl/ vs /kɔːl/ | The economy was based on **coal**. Please **call** me. |
| **grass / glass** | /ɡrɑːs/ vs /ɡlɑːs/ | The **grass** is yellow. A **glass** of water. |

Say each pair slowly, then in the sentence, then at normal speed. The difference is the first consonant and the vowel length — both words in a pair share the same ending, which is why they get swapped under pressure.

### E. Four Rules That Cover Most of the Errors Above

1. **After a modal, use the base verb:** will **go**, can **decide**, should **be**.
2. **Suggest ≠ advise:** **suggest that I go** / **advise me to go**.
3. **Regions take “the”:** in **the** south of China; in **the** north.
4. **One story, one time frame:** set the past in your first sentence and stay there.
