## 1. Solutions to Environmental Pollution

This section continues the pollution topic with a shift in focus: from describing the problem to **proposing solutions**. In English, a solution answer is much stronger when each measure is paired with the mechanism it works through.

### Reported Proposals

| Area | Reported proposal | Mechanism worth naming |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Industry** | Relocate factories to **the suburbs or outskirts**, away from cities. | **Spatial separation**: keeping emissions away from residential areas reduces residents' exposure. |
| **Transport** | Promote **electric vehicles** and **public transport** — buses and subways — to cut private car use. | Fewer cars on the road → lower **exhaust emissions** per person. |
| **Energy** | Use **renewable energy** for power generation: hydropower, wind power, solar. | Replaces **fossil fuels**, so pollution is prevented at the source rather than cleaned up later. |
| **Waste** | Provide more **rubbish bins**, and adopt the Scandinavian bottle-recycling reward model (Denmark, Sweden). | **Economic incentives**: paying people to return containers raises participation. |

**Vocabulary clarifications:**

- **Wind power** is generated by **wind turbines**. A **windmill** traditionally grinds grain or pumps water; using it for electricity is understandable but imprecise.
- **Hydropower**, not “water power”. Likewise **solar power / solar energy**.
- **Renewable energy** (can be naturally replenished: wind, solar, hydro) is more precise than **clean energy** (a broader, looser term that can include nuclear).
- **The suburbs** and **the outskirts** are both plural, both take **the**, and both mean the outer edge of a city. **A suburb** refers to one specific outer district.
- For the pollution itself: **smoke emissions** works, but **factory emissions**, **industrial emissions**, or **air pollutants** are more natural. **Exhaust fumes / vehicle emissions** for traffic.

### Fact-check note 1: the Scandinavian recycling model — added study material

The reported description is essentially correct, with two refinements.

**How it actually works.** In both countries the money is a **deposit** you pay when you buy the drink, not a bonus paid later. The container carries a deposit marking; you return it to a **reverse vending machine**, usually at a supermarket, and get your money back — typically as a **voucher or receipt** you cash at the till or deduct from your shopping, rather than as coins.

- **Denmark:** the deposit is called **pant**; the system is operated by **Dansk Retursystem**. The operator reports a return rate of about **93%** for deposit-marked packaging.
- **Sweden:** operated by **Returpack / Pantamera**. Deposits were **1–2 SEK**, with increases to **2–3 SEK** announced for cans and PET bottles. The operator reported that **over 3 billion** PET bottles and aluminium cans were returned in **2025**, an average of about **283 containers per person**, with a return rate of **88.4%**.

**How to say it accurately in English:**

> Denmark and Sweden both run deposit-return schemes. You pay a small deposit when you buy a drink, and you get it back when you return the bottle or can to a machine in a supermarket. Because people get money back, the return rates are very high — around 90% in both countries. That's a good example of using an economic incentive to change behaviour.

*Sources: [Dansk Retursystem — The Danish Deposit and Return System](https://danskretursystem.dk/en/dansk-retursystem-english/); [State of Green — The Danish deposit & return system](https://stateofgreen.com/en/solutions/the-danish-deposit-return-system-for-recycling-drink-cans-and-bottles/); [Packaging Europe — Over 3 billion PET bottles and aluminium cans returned through Swedish DRS in 2025](https://packagingeurope.com/news/over-3-billion-pet-bottles-and-aluminium-cans-returned-through-swedish-drs-in-2025/14072.article); [Packaging Europe — Deposits set to increase for PET and aluminium beverage packaging in Sweden](https://packagingeurope.com/news/deposits-set-to-increase-for-pet-and-aluminium-beverage-packaging-in-sweden/12372.article).*

**Added model answer — a full solution response:**

> In my opinion, we can reduce pollution in three ways. **First, at the source:** many factories have been moved to the outskirts of cities, so their emissions are further away from where people live. **Second, in transport and energy:** electric vehicles and public transport should be promoted, because fewer private cars means fewer exhaust fumes. Power generation should shift from coal to renewable sources such as hydropower, wind and solar. **Third, in waste management:** cities need more rubbish bins and clearer sorting rules. I'd also copy the deposit-return systems used in Denmark and Sweden, where people get their money back for returning bottles and cans. **Overall**, the most effective measures are the ones that make the clean choice the easy and cheap choice.

## 2. IELTS-Style Speaking Drills

**Added study framework — the two-minute talk.** These drills resemble IELTS Speaking Part 2: you receive a topic, prepare briefly, then speak for one to two minutes. The reliable structure is the same four-step plan:

1. **Frame:** “I'd like to talk about…”
2. **Group:** cover each point on the cue card in order — **who / what / when / why**.
3. **Support:** one concrete detail or short story per point.
4. **Land:** “The reason it matters to me is…”

**Timing tip:** Spend your preparation time choosing your three groups and one detail for each — not writing full sentences. A written sentence you cannot remember mid-talk causes a stall; a remembered detail does not.

### 2.1 Drill One: A Recent Gift

**Reported content:** The gift came from the learner's **best friend from university** — described as outgoing, easy-going, and strong in scientific research; they studied for **a course** together. The gift was a **university souvenir**, given at the **graduation ceremony** when the friend completed her studies and decided to continue learning elsewhere. Because the friend went abroad and has not returned, and future meetings are likely to be rare, the souvenir has become a **precious memory** symbolising their friendship.

**Language points for this topic:**

| Expression | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **a souvenir / a keepsake / a memento** | A keepsake or memento is something kept for the memory attached to it. |
| **a token of our friendship** | A slightly formal, warm way to describe the meaning. |
| **at the graduation ceremony** | The event; **at** is the right preposition. |
| **pursue a PhD / do a PhD** | Not “get a PhD degree” — **a PhD** already means the degree. |
| **She chose to study abroad.** | Past decision → **chose**, not **choose**. |
| **She hasn't come back to China since.** | **Come back / return** supplies the verb that **back** alone cannot. |
| **There's little chance of seeing her again.** | Uncountable **chance** (probability) → **little**; countable **chances** (occasions) → **fewer**. |
| **We keep in touch by video call.** | Continuing contact; **keep in touch**, not “stay in touch” only — both are correct, but **keep in touch** is more common for ongoing contact. |

**Added model answer:**

> I'd like to talk about a gift I received recently — a university souvenir from my best friend. We studied together. She's outgoing and easy-going, and she was also very good at research. At our graduation ceremony, she gave me this souvenir, because she had decided to pursue a further qualification abroad. She hasn't come back to China since then, so there's little chance of us meeting again soon. That's why this small souvenir means so much to me — it's a token of our friendship, and it reminds me of the time we spent studying together. I hope she'll come back soon.

### 2.2 Drill Two: An Interesting Hobby — Long-Distance Running

**Practice topic:** Long-distance running can be described through breathing technique, pacing and endurance. The example below is an illustrative hobby answer, not the instructor's personal history.

**Language points for this topic:**

- **breath control / breathing technique** — note the noun is **breath** /breθ/, the verb is **breathe** /briːð/.
- **pace yourself** — control your speed so you do not tire too early.
- **conserve / save energy**
- **build up stamina / endurance**
- **the final stretch** — the last part of a race; **a sprint finish** — running fast at the end.
- **warm up / cool down**
- **a personal best** — your fastest time so far.
- **It's beginner-friendly.** / **It doesn't need any special equipment.**

**Added model answer:**

> The hobby I find most interesting is long-distance running. What makes it interesting isn't the speed — it's the strategy. The most important skill is breath control: if your breathing is steady, you can keep going much longer. You also have to pace yourself, because if you run too fast at the start, you have nothing left at the end. Experienced runners usually conserve energy and save it for the final stretch. I enjoy jogging myself, mainly because it's beginner-friendly and I can do it in my spare time after lunch.

### 2.3 Drill Three: A Useful Website

**Reported content:** The recommendation was **“Hacker News”** — the summary notes the name was misheard in transcription as *Hiking* or *Hacking news*. It was described as a news site aggregating the latest developments in **computer science, AI, politics** and other fields, used daily to follow frontier industry information, and said to integrate material from sources such as **Twitter and blogs** through **categories and tags**, saving time on searching.

**Language points for this topic:**

- **aggregate / an aggregator** — to collect content from many sources in one place.
- **keep up with trends in this field** — not “know the trend of this word”.
- **the latest news / the latest updates** — more natural than “the newest things”.
- **I don't need to check every website.** — or **I needn't check every website.** Never **needn't to**.
- **It saves me a lot of time.** / **It's a time-saver.**
- **browse / scroll through / subscribe to**

### Fact-check note 2: Hacker News — added study material

Three corrections are worth knowing before you describe this site in English.

1. **The name.** It is **Hacker News** — no -ing. In this community, **hacker** originally means a skilled programmer who enjoys building things, not a criminal. Pronunciation: **hacker** /ˈhækə/ rhymes with *back*; **hiking** /ˈhaɪkɪŋ/ rhymes with *like*. The two are not close in vowel sound, so slow the vowel down rather than speaking faster.
2. **What it covers.** It is a social news site created by **Paul Graham** in **2007** and run by the startup accelerator **Y Combinator**. Its focus is **computer science and entrepreneurship** — technology, startups, programming, and AI — with occasional wider topics. Describing it as covering “politics” overstates its range.
3. **How it works.** It does **not** automatically pull content from Twitter. Users **submit links** from anywhere — blogs, news sites, research pages, company announcements — and the community votes. Stories are ranked by points and age, which is why the front page shows what readers currently find most interesting. Sections include **new**, **comments**, **ask**, **show**, and **jobs**.

**How to say it accurately in English:**

> I'd like to recommend a website called Hacker News, which is run by Y Combinator. It focuses on computer science and startups. It isn't written by editors — users submit links to articles and blog posts, and other users vote on them, so the front page shows what the tech community finds most interesting that day. I browse it every morning, because it helps me keep up with trends in this field without checking dozens of websites one by one. It saves me a lot of time.

*Sources: [Hacker News — official site](https://news.ycombinator.com/); [Hacker News FAQ — Y Combinator](https://news.ycombinator.com/newsfaq.html); [The Evolution of Hacker News — TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013-05-18/the-evolution-of-hacker-news/).*

## 3. Gap Fillers and Logical Connectors

### Reported guidance on gap fillers

**Reported content:** Gap fillers were described as a way to **cover silence while thinking**, buying time to organise language — better than complete silence. Examples given: **Well**, **I think that**, **To be honest**, **Frankly speaking**, **It seems to me**, **Probably**.

**Added clarification:** Fillers work like punctuation for the listener — they signal “more is coming”. But they only help in moderation. Repeating **well** every sentence becomes noticeable and can lower a fluency score. The stronger technique is to **vary** them and to match the filler to your purpose:

| Purpose | Natural fillers |
| --- | --- |
| Buying a second to think | **Well…**, **Let me think…**, **That's a good question.** |
| Softening an opinion | **It seems to me…**, **I'd say…**, **As far as I know…** |
| Being direct | **To be honest…**, **Frankly speaking…**, **Actually…** |
| Expressing uncertainty | **Probably**, **I suppose**, **I'm not sure, but…** |
| Recovering from a mistake | **Let me put it another way…**, **What I mean is…** |

### Reported guidance on logical connectors

| Function | Reported connectors | Added note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conclusion / summary** | Finally, Eventually, In the end, In short, Briefly, As a conclusion | **Eventually** means “after a long time”, not “finally, to sum up”. For a summary use **In short**, **To sum up**, **Overall**, or **All in all**. |
| **Result** | So, As a result, Consequently, Thus | **So** is neutral and spoken; **consequently** and **thus** are formal and mostly written. |
| **Cause** | Because, Since, As | These introduce a **clause** (subject + verb). |
| **Cause with a noun** | Due to, Because of | These are followed by a **noun or noun phrase** only. |

**The rule that catches most learners — added study material:**

> **because / since / as + subject + verb** → “The air was bad **because the city depended on coal**.”  
> **because of / due to + noun phrase** → “The air was bad **because of coal**.” / “**Due to the coal industry**, the air was bad.”

**Common errors to avoid:** “because of the city depended on coal” ✗ · “due to it rained” ✗ · “as a result of he was late” ✗.

**Connectors in action — added example:**

> Air quality in my hometown used to be poor, mainly **because** the local economy depended on coal. **As a result**, smoke from factories was a daily problem. **Since** many factories have been relocated to the outskirts, the situation has improved. **In short**, the change came from separating industry from residential areas.

## 4. Pronunciation: Linking, Weak Forms, and the /r/ Difference

### Reported points on natural speech

- **Final sound omission:** native speakers often drop the **t** at the end of words, so “favorite sport” was reported as sounding like “favorite spor”.
- **Weak forms and contractions:** **I'll** for *I will*, **don't** for *do not*, and a weakened **d** in “good morning”.

**Added clarification — what is really happening:**

| Phenomenon | Accurate description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Elision of /t/ and /d/** | A final /t/ or /d/ is often dropped or replaced by a glottal stop **when the next word begins with a consonant**. | *just made* → “jus' made”; *last night*; *next day*; *good morning* → the /d/ disappears before /m/. |
| **Flapping (American)** | /t/ between two vowels sounds like a quick /d/. | *water* → “wadder”; *better* → “bedder”. |
| **Contractions** | Short forms are the default in speech, not a casual exception. | *I'll, don't, it's, there's, we've*. |
| **Weak forms** | Grammar words shrink to /ə/-type sounds. | *of* → /əv/, *to* → /tə/, *and* → /ən/, *for* → /fə/, *can* → /kən/. |
| **Linking** | A word ending in a consonant joins smoothly to a word starting with a vowel. | *turn_it_off*, *an_interesting_hobby*. |

**Important:** “Favorite sport” at the end of a sentence normally keeps its /t/, or becomes a glottal stop. The /t/ really disappears when followed by another consonant. So the reported example describes a real rule, applied to a slightly wrong example — trust the rule.

**Practice tip:** Do not try to speak faster. Weak forms and linking come from **reducing effort on grammar words**, not from rushing the important words. Stress the content words — *factories, outskirts, emissions* — and let *of, to, and, the* shrink.

### American vs British /r/: rhotic and non-rhotic

**Reported point:** American English pronounces the **r** clearly (**park**, **car**), while British English usually does not pronounce it, or weakens it before a consonant. The example sentence given was **“The bar is quite far, we need a car”**, in which the British /r/ sounds were reported as almost inaudible.

**Added study material — the correct terms and one important correction:**

- **Rhotic** = /r/ is pronounced after a vowel. **Most American English is rhotic**: *car* /kɑːr/, *park* /pɑːrk/.
- **Non-rhotic** = /r/ after a vowel is not pronounced, and the vowel is usually longer. **Most standard British English (RP) is non-rhotic**: *car* /kɑː/, *park* /pɑːk/.
- **Correction:** It is not true that British English never pronounces /r/. Several British accents **are** rhotic — Scottish, West Country, and parts of Lancashire and Northern Ireland. Say **“most British accents”** or **“standard British English”**, not **“British English”**.
- **Linking /r/:** a non-rhotic speaker pronounces /r/ when the next word starts with a vowel. So *far* alone → /fɑː/, but *far away* → /fɑːr əˈweɪ/. The /r/ comes back.

**Drill — say each pair both ways:**

| Word or phrase | Rhotic (most American) | Non-rhotic (RP) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| car | /kɑːr/ | /kɑː/ |
| park | /pɑːrk/ | /pɑːk/ |
| The bar is quite far. | …/bɑːr/ … /fɑːr/ | …/bɑː/ … /fɑː/ |
| far away | /fɑːr əˈweɪ/ | /fɑːr əˈweɪ/ *(linking /r/ returns)* |
| water | /ˈwɔːtər/ | /ˈwɔːtə/ |

### The Chinese dialect analogy

**Reported point:** The tutor compared the /r/ difference to Chinese dialects — northern varieties often add a **retroflex / 儿化** ending (as in “金儿”), while southern varieties generally do not.

**Added clarification:** The analogy is useful for remembering **which side pronounces the r-colouring** — north does, south doesn't — and it maps neatly onto American (rhotic) versus standard British (non-rhotic). But keep two limits in mind: Chinese **儿化** adds an r-colour to a vowel at the end of a syllable and is optional and regional, while English /r/ is a full consonant that changes meaning between words (**car** vs **ca'**, **wetter** vs **weda**). Use the analogy as a memory hook, not as a linguistic explanation.

**Neither accent is “correct”.** Choose one and stay consistent within an answer; mixing them mid-sentence is what listeners notice.

## 5. Grammar and Expression Review

The first column adapts **reported wording from the supplied error analysis**. It is not audio-verified. Revisions preserve the likely intended meaning.

### A. The Gift Topic

| Reported wording | Corrected or more natural version | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “We discussed about the use of electric cars…” | **We discussed the use of electric cars…** | **Discuss** is transitive — no **about**. Same with **mention**, **enter**, **marry**. |
| “…she choose to get a PhD degree…” | **…she chose to do a PhD…** | Past decision → **chose**. **A PhD** already means the degree, so “PhD degree” is redundant. |
| “…get a PhD degree in French.” | **…do a PhD in France.** | **France** = the country; **French** = the language or the people. |
| “She has have much power on the research work.” | **She has great influence in research.** / **She's very capable in research.** | Never **has have**. The collocations are **influence on/over** or **ability/capability in**. |
| “She never back the back to China.” | **She never came back to China.** / **She hasn't come back to China.** | **Back** is an adverb; the sentence needs a verb — **come back** or **return**. |
| “There are less chance for me to meet her.” | **There is little chance of seeing her again.** / **There are fewer chances to meet her.** | Uncountable **chance** (probability) → **little** + singular verb; countable **chances** (occasions) → **fewer**. **Less** does not pair with a singular countable noun. |
| “Appreciate she will come backsong.” | **I hope she'll come back soon.** | Reconstructed from context. **Appreciate** means “am grateful for” or “understand” — it never means “hope”. It also takes a noun or **-ing**: **I appreciate your help.** |

### B. The Website Topic

| Reported wording | Corrected or more natural version | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “It's news… hacking news.” | **It's a tech news site called Hacker News.** | Name and pronunciation: **hacker** /ˈhækə/, not **hiking** /ˈhaɪkɪŋ/. |
| “The website always provided newest things.” | **The website always provides the latest news.** | A site's regular function → **present simple**. **The latest news/updates**, not “newest things”. |
| “To know the trend of the This word.” | **To keep up with trends in this field.** | Reconstructed: **word** → **field** (or **world**). The natural collocation is **keep up with trends**. |
| “I needn't to check every website.” | **I don't need to check every website.** / **I needn't check every website.** | **Needn't + base verb** (no **to**), or **don't need to + base verb**. |

### C. General Grammar and Vocabulary

| Reported wording | Corrected or more natural version | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Many factories moved to another city.” | **Many factories have moved to other cities.** | **Present perfect** links past action to present results. Plural **factories** → plural **other cities**; **another** means one more. |
| “A suburbs.” / “In suburb.” | **The suburbs.** / **In the suburbs.** | Usually plural with **the**. **A suburb** = one specific outer district. |
| “Outskirt of the city.” | **The outskirts of the city.** | **Outskirts** is virtually always plural, with **the**. |
| “By water power or other clean energy.” | **Using hydropower or other renewable energy sources.** | **Hydropower** is the standard term; **renewable energy** is more precise than **clean energy**. |

### D. Five Patterns That Cover Most of the Errors Above

1. **Discuss / mention + object** — no preposition.
2. **France (country) vs French (language, people).**
3. **Needn't + base verb** or **don't need to + base verb** — never **needn't to**.
4. **Back needs a verb:** come back, go back, return.
5. **Little (uncountable) vs fewer (countable):** little chance, fewer chances.
