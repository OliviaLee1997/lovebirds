## 1. Restaurant Role-play: Polite Requests

**Reported activity:** the learner played a customer and the tutor played a waiter in a Chinese restaurant scenario. The order included coconut pizza, pineapple, fruit salad, and juice.

### Useful Expressions

| Purpose | Natural expression | Usage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ask for the menu | **Could I see the menu, please?** | Also: **Could you please show me the menu?** |
| Order food | **I'd like a coconut pizza, please.** | **I'd like to have…** is also correct. |
| Order a drink | **Could I have a glass of juice, please?** | A polite request from the customer. |
| Offer something | **Would you like something to drink?** | An offer or question about preference, often from the waiter. |
| Accept an offer | **Yes, please.** | Short and polite. |
| Decline an offer | **No, thank you.** | Short and polite. |
| Ask the price | **How much does the meal cost?** | Grammatically correct. Also: **How much is the meal?** |
| Request the bill | **Could we have the bill, please?** | In American English, **check** is common. |
| Ask the total | **What's the total?** | Asks how much needs to be paid. |

**I'd like = I would like.** Follow it with a noun or **to + verb**:

> I'd like **some juice**.  
> I'd like **to order**.

**Could you + base verb** asks someone to do something:

> Could you **bring** the menu, please?

**Would you like…?** offers something; **I'd like…** expresses your own preference. They serve different roles in a conversation.

**Politeness clarification:** **I want…** is grammatical, but it can sound blunt when ordering. **I'd like…** and **Could I have…?** are ordinary polite English, not expressions restricted to formal situations. Tone and **please** also matter.

### Edited Practice Dialogue

This is a reconstructed model based on the reported order, not a transcript.

> **Waiter:** Hello. Would you like to see the menu?  
> **The learner:** Yes, please.  
> **Waiter:** What would you like to order?  
> **The learner:** I'd like a coconut pizza, some pineapple, and a fruit salad, please.  
> **Waiter:** Would you like something to drink?  
> **The learner:** Could I have a glass of juice, please?  
> **Waiter:** Of course. Anything else?  
> **The learner:** No, thank you.  
> **Later — the learner:** Could I have the bill, please?

**Asking the price** and **requesting the bill** are different purposes. “How much does the meal cost?” asks for a price; “Could I have the bill?” asks the waiter to bring the bill.

## 2. Cook, Cooker, Chef, and Restaurant Staff

| Word | Meaning | Added example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **cook** — noun | A person who prepares food, professionally or at home | My grandfather is a good **cook**. |
| **cook** — verb | Prepare food using heat | I **cook** dinner at home. |
| **cooker** — noun | A cooking appliance; in British English, commonly a stove or range | The electric **cooker** isn't working. |
| **chef** — noun | A professional cook, often trained or responsible for a kitchen | The restaurant's **chef** created this dish. |
| **waiter / waitress** | A person who serves customers at tables | The **waiter** brought the menu. |
| **server** | A gender-neutral term for someone serving customers, especially in American English | Our **server** was helpful. |

**Remember: a cook is a person; a cooker is an appliance.** An **oven** is specifically the enclosed heated space used for baking or roasting; it is not an exact synonym for every use of **cooker**. Compounds such as **rice cooker** name particular appliances.

The ending **-er** does not always indicate a profession: compare **teacher**, **cooker**, and **computer**.

## 3. Personality, Judgement, and Art Vocabulary

### Personality and Judgement

| Word | Part of speech and meaning | Added example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **punctual** | Adjective: arriving or doing things at the agreed time | She's punctual; she always arrives on time. |
| **arrogant** | Adjective: acting as though you are better or more important than others | He sounded arrogant when he dismissed everyone else's ideas. |
| **arrogance** | Noun: the quality of being arrogant | His arrogance made teamwork difficult. |
| **proud** | Adjective: feeling satisfaction; sometimes showing excessive self-importance | I'm proud of my achievement. |
| **pride** | Noun: satisfaction or a sense of self-worth; sometimes excessive self-importance | She takes pride in her work. |
| **intellectual** | Adjective: relating to thought and ideas; noun: a person deeply engaged with ideas and study | I enjoy intellectual discussions. / She is an intellectual. |
| **intelligent** | Adjective: able to learn, understand, and reason well | She's an intelligent student. |
| **prejudice** | Noun: an unfair preconceived judgement, often about a person or group | We should challenge prejudice. |
| **prejudiced** | Adjective: holding such unfair judgements | The comment was prejudiced. |
| **bias** | Noun: an inclination or systematic tendency that can affect judgement or results | The study may contain sampling bias. |
| **biased** | Adjective: unfairly favouring one side, or systematically skewed | That report gives a biased account. |

**Important distinctions:**

- **Proud** is not automatically negative. **I'm proud of my award** expresses healthy satisfaction; **arrogant** implies a sense of superiority. Selfishness may coexist with arrogance, but it is not the word's definition.
- **Intellectual** is related to **intellect**, not **electro-**. It is not simply a label for someone with a high IQ. **Intelligent** is usually better when you mean “smart.”
- **Prejudice** and **bias** overlap, but **bias** also has technical meanings. In statistics or machine learning, a bias can be a systematic tendency or, in some contexts, a model parameter; it does not always mean social prejudice.

**Reported memory connection:** the learner linked **prejudice** to *Pride and Prejudice*. The tutor encouraged gathering enough information before judging people.

### Art and Personal Experience

**Art** can include painting, sculpture, cinema, theatre, dance, and opera. **Artistic** describes creative ability, interest, or qualities associated with art.

**Anonymised practice example:**

> I wouldn't describe myself as particularly artistic, but I enjoy **learning about different forms of art**. In this practice description of a group photo, **I'm in the middle**.

**Useful structures:**

- **Be good at + noun:** I'm not very good at **art**.
- **Be good at + -ing:** She's good at **painting**.
- **Be interested in + noun / -ing:** I'm interested in **opera / learning about opera**.
- **A form of art:** Opera is **a form of art**.
- **A private institution:** An organisation outside the public sector; **a private training school** is more specific if that describes the organisation.

**Private situation** means a personal or non-public circumstance; it does not mean an institution.

## 4. Grammar Focus: Clear Sentence Structures

### A. Avoid Unnecessary Subject Repetition

**Reported wording:** “Most of them em they are em em the gallery's topic is about paint...” 

For a clear neutral sentence, choose one subject expression:

> **Most of them are about painting.**  
> **The galleries mostly focus on painting.**

Use **painting**, not **paint**, when referring to the art form. **Paint** is the material. Use **them** only when the listener knows what it refers to.

**Grammar illustration; no personal background is implied:**

> “My father, he is a doctor.”   
> Clear neutral version: **My father is a doctor.**

**Clarification:** English does allow conversational topic structures such as “My father, he's a doctor,” and sentences may have coordinated subjects or several clauses. The useful editing rule is **avoid an unnecessary repeated subject in a simple neutral clause**, not “every sentence can have only one subject.”

### B. Questions with Be vs. Do/Does

| Statement | Question | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opera **is** a form of art. | **Is opera** a form of art? | Move **be** before the subject. |
| She **likes** opera. | **Does she like** opera? | Use **does + subject + base verb**. |
| They **work** here. | **Do they work** here? | Use **do + subject + base verb**. |

Correct **“Does the opera is a kind of art?”** to **“Is opera a kind of art?”** Use **opera** without **the** when discussing the art form generally.

### C. Want to Improve a Skill

> **I want to improve my motorcycle riding skills.**

**Want + to + base verb:** want **to improve**, want **to practise**, want **to learn**.

Use **ride a motorcycle** and **motorcycle riding skills** as the usual expressions. **Drive a car** and **driving skills** fit cars.

### D. Past Events and Experience Continuing Until Now

> **I won an award in [year].**  
> **My classmate interpreted for me during the competition.**  
> **I attended her wedding last month.**

A finished time such as **in [year]** or **last month** normally calls for the simple past when reporting the event. Relevant past forms include **win → won**, **be → was/were**, and **attend → attended**.

However, a story need not remain in one tense throughout:

> **At the time, my English wasn't very good. Now I find technical vocabulary challenging.**

The tense changes because the time changes.

**“I have been driving for four years” is already correct.** It describes experience or repeated activity over a period leading up to now; it does not mean nonstop driving. Keep these meanings distinct:

- **I've been driving for four years.** — Driving experience over that period.
- **I've had my driving licence for four years.** — How long I have held the licence.
- **I've been learning to drive for four years.** — A learning process over that period; this changes the original meaning.

## 5. Academic Achievement and Career Interests

**Reported background:** the learner won an award at the **student project** competition in another city in **[year]**. The competition involved technical project work. Her English was limited at the time, so a classmate helped her communicate. The learner later attended that classmate's wedding.

**Polished model answer:**

> In [year], I won an award at the student project competition in another city. The competition focused on technical project work. My English wasn't very good at the time, so a classmate **interpreted for me**, translating from Chinese into English. Although I did well in biology, I realised at university that I **wasn't interested in research work**. I now find it challenging to discuss technical topics in English, especially in computer science and biology.

**Interpret** usually refers to spoken or signed communication; **translate** often refers to written text, though it is also used broadly for language conversion. **She translated from Chinese into English for me** is acceptable; **interpreted for me** is more precise for live conversation.

**Wedding vs. marriage:**

- **Wedding:** the ceremony or celebration — **I attended her wedding.**
- **Marriage:** the relationship or legal union — **They have a happy marriage.**

**Added technical vocabulary:**

| Term | Brief meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **a technical subject** | Deliberately modifying an organism's genetic material |
| **yeast** | A type of fungus used in baking, fermentation, and research |
| **research** | Systematic investigation; normally uncountable in general usage |
| **technical terminology** | Specialised words used in a particular field |
| **computer science** | The study of computation and computing systems |

Useful clarification requests: **“Could you explain that term?”**, **“How do you pronounce this word?”**, and **“Let me explain it in simpler terms.”**

## 6. Internet Habits and Digital Well-being

**Reported discussion:** the learner uses Quora, Xiaohongshu (Little Red Book), Instagram, and YouTube. She described herself as **addicted to social media** and said she sometimes felt tired and regretful after spending too much time online. This is her reported description, not a clinical assessment.

**Polished model answer:**

> I use the internet every day, mainly for social media. Sometimes I **spend too much time scrolling**. Afterwards, I feel tired and **regret spending so much time online**. I'd like to manage my screen time better.

| Pattern | Added example |
| --- | --- |
| **Be addicted to + noun / -ing** | I'm addicted to social media. / I'm addicted to checking my phone. |
| **Spend time + -ing** | I spend too much time scrolling. |
| **Regret + -ing** | I regret staying online so late. |
| **Feel + adjective** | I feel tired and regretful afterwards. |
| **Cut down on + noun / -ing** | I want to cut down on screen time. |

Here, **to** in **addicted to** is a preposition, so use **checking**, not **check**.

**Reported view about children:** Both participants favoured limiting children's phone use and allowing brief periods with downloaded educational content, such as English cartoons, to reduce online distractions. This is a classroom opinion, not a universal rule about children's needs.

**Edited opinion:**

> I think children should use phones **for short periods**. Downloaded educational videos could help them learn while reducing distractions from browsing online.

## 7. Holiday Plans and Motorcycle Regulations

**Anonymised practice plan:** Describe practising a skill during free time, without disclosing a personal itinerary.

> **I want to practise riding my motorcycle during my free time before I go back to work.**

**Reported regulatory discussion:** the learner described restrictions on motorcycles in certain urban areas and situations where transport by truck was necessary.

**Careful edited wording:**

> Motorcycles are restricted in some city areas in China. Depending on the route and local rules, I may need to **transport my motorcycle by truck**.

Do not turn this discussion into a claim that **all intercity motorcycle journeys in China require a truck**. Restrictions depend on the location and road. **Flatbed truck** is appropriate if the vehicle has a flat, open loading platform; otherwise, **truck** or **motorcycle transporter** is sufficient.

### E. Speaking Practice

1. **Restaurant:** Order two dishes and a drink, respond to an offer, and request the bill.
2. **Personal achievement:** Explain what you achieved, when it happened, and who helped you. Then describe how you feel about it now.
3. **Art:** Describe something artistic you have tried or would like to try. Use **good at**, **interested in**, and **a form of art**.
4. **Internet habits:** Name the platforms you use, explain one benefit and one drawback, and describe one habit you would like to change.
5. **Technical English:** Explain a familiar computer science concept in simple English. Ask your partner which term needs clarification.

**Optional AI practice prompt:** “Be my English conversation partner. Start with a restaurant role-play, then ask about a past achievement and my internet habits. Ask one question at a time. After each answer, identify one useful correction, explain it briefly, and let me try again. Do not label correct expressions as errors.”
