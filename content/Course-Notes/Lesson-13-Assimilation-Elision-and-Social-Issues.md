## 1. Connected Speech: Three Different Processes

Connected speech involves coordinating sounds across word boundaries. Smooth speech does not require deleting every final consonant or speaking quickly.

| Process | What changes? | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Assimilation** | A sound changes to become more like a nearby sound. | The /n/ in **green park** can become [m] before /p/. |
| **Elision** | A sound is omitted. | The /d/ in **second choice** may be omitted in casual speech. |
| **Unreleased stop** | The closure is made, but there is no separately audible release burst. | The /t/ in **cigarette paper** may be retained without an audible release. |

**Notation:** / / indicates phonemes; [ ] indicates a possible phonetic realization. Examples below illustrate possible pronunciations, not obligatory rules for every accent or speaking style.

## 2. Three Types of Assimilation

### Regressive assimilation: the next sound affects the earlier sound

An alveolar nasal /n/ can change its place of articulation before another consonant:

| Phrase | Possible change | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **green park** | /n/ → [m] before /p/ | The lips close in preparation for /p/. |
| **ten bucks** | /n/ → [m] before /b/ | The nasal remains; it becomes bilabial. |
| **in my** | /n/ → [m] before /m/ | The adjacent nasals may merge into one continuous nasal gesture. |
| **ten cars** | /n/ → [ŋ] before /k/ | The nasal may move to the back of the mouth before a velar consonant. |

**Correction to the supplied summary:** “i-my” and “te-bucks” are misleading practice spellings if they encourage deleting all nasal sound. These examples commonly illustrate **nasal place assimilation**, not simply loss of /n/.

**Added practice sentences:**

- We walked through the **green park**.
- The ticket costs **ten bucks**.
- I keep my notes **in my** bag.
- There were **ten cars** outside.

### Progressive assimilation: the earlier sound affects the later sound

The pronunciation of the regular plural ending depends on the preceding sound:

| Environment | Ending | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| After a voiceless non-sibilant consonant | /s/ | **cats**, **books** |
| After a voiced non-sibilant sound | /z/ | **dogs**, **bags**, **days** |
| After a sibilant, such as /s, z, ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/ | /ɪz/, with accent-dependent variation | **buses**, **watches** |

This is a useful example of sound-conditioned suffix variation. Do not generalize it into a rule that every written **s** becomes voiced after a voiced sound.

**Please** already has lexical /z/: /pliːz/. It is not a clear example of an optional /s/ becoming /z/ during connected speech.

**Added practice:** **Two cats, three dogs, and four buses.** Listen for three different endings.

### Coalescent or mutual assimilation: two sounds combine

- **Did you…?** /d/ + /j/ can become [dʒ]: a possible casual form is [ˈdɪdʒə].
- **Would you…?** /d/ + /j/ can become [dʒ]: a possible casual form is [ˈwʊdʒə].
- **Don't you…?** /t/ + /j/ can become [tʃ].

**Didja/wouldja** are pronunciation reminders, not normal spellings in formal writing. The resulting sound in **did you** is [dʒ], not [dz].

**Added practice sentences:**

- **Did you** enjoy the lesson?
- **Would you** like a cup of tea?
- **Don't you** remember that example?

These processes occur in both British and American English, with variation by accent, speaking style, and phrase. The /dr/ sequence in **drive** can have an affricated realization in several accents, but it is not the same environment as /d/ + /j/ in **did you**.

## 3. Final Stops: What to Retain and What May Reduce

The classroom summary says final /t/ and /d/ are usually omitted before consonants. That is **too broad**. Possible outcomes include retention, an unreleased stop, assimilation, glottal reinforcement or replacement in some accents, and elision in suitable clusters.

### Examples from the supplied material

| Phrase | More accurate learning point |
| --- | --- |
| **basket maker** | Final /t/ can be unreleased, assimilated toward a bilabial stop before /m/, or reduced in some styles. A /t/ → [p] realization is assimilation, not deletion; it is not compulsory. |
| **best man** | The /t/ in the cluster /st m/ may be elided. The following consonant is /m/, not /n/. |
| **second choice** | The /d/ in /nd tʃ/ may be elided in connected speech. |
| **cigarette paper** | /t/ before /p/ may have no audible release; this does not prove /t/ has disappeared. |
| **circuit board** | /t/ before /b/ may be unreleased or otherwise reduced, depending on accent and style. |
| **coconut butter** | The word-boundary /t b/ sequence allows similar variation; do not force one casual realization. |
| **cup of tea** | A common reduction is weak **of** /əv/, sometimes with /v/ omitted. This is not an example of obligatory final /t/ or /d/ deletion. |
| **and** | May be weak /ən/ or /n/ in suitable contexts; use a fuller form when stressed or spoken carefully. |
| **the surprise** | Standard **the** before /s/ is /ðə/. The /ð/ is followed by the vowel /ə/, so this phrase is not a straightforward adjacent /ð/ + /s/ cluster. |

**Practice principle:** Say the phrase clearly first. Then repeat it smoothly without inserting extra vowels between consonants. Learn casual reductions by listening and imitation rather than deleting letters mechanically.

## 5. The learner's Grammar Review: What Is Actually an Error?

### A. Clear errors, if the reported wording is accurate

| Reported wording | Correction | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “the status of… white people… are more higher… than… black people” | **The status of white people is higher than that of Black people.** | **Status** is singular → **is**. **Higher**, not **more higher**. Compare status with status: **that of**. This is a grammatical repair, not an endorsement or a precise historical claim. |
| “They have a honor of being a white people.” | **They take pride in being white.** | **An honor**, because the initial **h** is silent; **people** is plural, so not **a white people** when referring to individuals. **Take pride in** is the intended collocation if the meaning is pride. |
| “We learned that on the history class.” | **We learned that in history class.** | **In class**, not **on class**. **In the history class** can be correct if a specific class is identified. |
| “I have heard a lot of histories about American in my history class.” | **I have learned a lot about American history in my history class.** | **History** is normally uncountable for the subject; **American** modifies **history**. **About America** also works. The original present perfect is not itself an error. |

For a completed course, say **I learned a lot about American history in school**. For learning up to now, **I have learned…** is appropriate. **Histories** is possible when referring to separate historical accounts, but that is probably not the intended meaning here.

### B. Cases the supplied analysis should not count as definite grammar errors

**1. “I have heard heard of that.” **

- This contains a spoken repetition, not a faulty tense structure.
- Clean version: **I have heard of that.**
- **I have never heard of that** changes the meaning to the opposite. Only use it if the recording confirms a negative meaning.

**2. “I know reason… I don't know the word Russia.” **

- Likely a vocabulary, listening, or transcription issue; a reliable grammar diagnosis is not possible from these words alone.
- Possible intended meaning: **I know the word racism, but I don't know the adjective.**
- If referring to an earlier gap in knowledge: **I knew the word racism, but I didn't know the adjective.**

**3. “They really think a white man's the…” **

- The utterance is unfinished. Its intended final structure is unknown.
- A useful completed sentence is **They wrongly believe that white people are superior to other racial groups.** This explicitly attributes and rejects the belief.
- Pauses and self-corrections affect fluency, but they are not automatically grammar errors.

**4. “White people consider themselves are more superior…”**

- The supplied analysis attributes this sentence to **another speaker**, so it should not be counted as the learner's confirmed error.
- The grammar patterns are **consider + object + adjective** and **believe + that-clause**: **They consider themselves superior** / **They believe that they are superior**.
- Use **superior to**, not **more superior than**, for this intended comparison.

### C. More precise wording for social issues

Avoid presenting racial superiority as a fact or attributing one belief to every member of a racial group. Specify the people, period, and claim you mean.

**Added model sentences:**

- **Some people wrongly believe that their racial group is superior to others.**
- **Under racial segregation in the United States, Black Americans were denied equal rights.**
- **We discussed racial discrimination in history class.**
- **I understand the general idea, but I don't yet have the vocabulary to explain it clearly.**

**Main grammar priorities:** **singular subjects**, **comparative forms**, **in class**, and **noun/adjective distinctions**. The source does not support counting every hesitation, transcription oddity, or tutor sentence as the learner's mistake.

## 6. Vocabulary for the Next Discussion — Added Study Material

| Word or expression | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **race** | A social category associated with perceived ancestry and physical characteristics. | People should not face discrimination because of their **race**. |
| **racial** | Related to race. | We discussed **racial inequality**. |
| **racism** | Racial prejudice and discrimination, including practices or systems that sustain racial inequality. | **Racism** can affect access to opportunities. |
| **racist** | Adjective for racist ideas/actions; also a noun for a person who holds racist beliefs. | That claim is **racist**. |
| **stereotype** | A generalized, oversimplified belief about a group. | A **stereotype** ignores individual differences. |
| **prejudice** | A preconceived judgement, often negative. | **Prejudice** can influence how people treat others. |
| **discrimination** | Unfair treatment based on identity or group membership. | The law prohibits racial **discrimination**. |
| **segregation** | Enforced separation of groups. | We learned about racial **segregation** in the United States. |
| **civil rights** | Rights to equal treatment and participation in civic life. | The movement fought for **civil rights**. |
| **equality** | Equal rights, status, or opportunities. | Education can support greater **equality**. |

**Useful collocations:** **discriminate against someone**, **be prejudiced against a group**, **challenge a stereotype**, **have equal rights**, **be superior to**.

## 7. Review Practice — Added Study Material

### A. Correct or clarify the sentences

1. The status of this group are more higher than that of the other group.
2. She received a honor for her work.
3. We learned about segregation on history class.
4. I learned a lot about American in school. *(You mean the country's history.)*
5. They consider themselves are superior. *(Correct the structure without endorsing the belief.)*
6. I have heard heard of that. *(Remove only the repetition.)*
7. Their argument is more superior than ours. *(Use the standard comparison pattern.)*
8. I know the noun racism, but I don't know its adjective form. *(Is this grammatical?)*

### B. Identify the process

1. /n/ becomes [m] in **green park**.
2. /d/ is omitted in **second choice**.
3. /d/ + /j/ becomes [dʒ] in **would you**.
4. The /t/ closure is retained but has no audible release in **cigarette paper**.
5. The plural ending is /z/ in **dogs**, after voiced /g/.

### C. Read aloud

Read each sentence carefully, then at a comfortable conversational speed. Do not force reductions.

1. **Would you** put it **in my** bag?
2. **Did you** walk through the **green park**?
3. The **best man** was my **second choice**.
4. I bought two **books** and three **bags**.
5. We learned about **racial discrimination in history class**.
6. I have heard of that, but I don't know much about it.

### Answer key

**A.**

1. The status of this group **is higher** than that of the other group.
2. She received **an honor** for her work.
3. We learned about segregation **in history class**.
4. I learned a lot about **American history** in school.
5. They **consider themselves superior**. This reports their judgement, not an objective fact.
6. **I have heard of that.**
7. Their argument is **superior to** ours. Also natural: **Their argument is better than ours.**
8. **Yes.** It is grammatical. The relevant adjective may be **racial** or **racist**, depending on meaning; they are not interchangeable.

**B.** 1. **Regressive place assimilation**; 2. **Elision**; 3. **Coalescent assimilation**; 4. **An unreleased stop, not necessarily deletion**; 5. **Sound-conditioned plural variation, illustrating progressive voicing agreement**.
