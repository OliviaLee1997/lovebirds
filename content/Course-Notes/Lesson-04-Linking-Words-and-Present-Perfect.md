## 1. Linking Words: Make the Relationship Clear

Choose the relationship first: **contrast**, **addition**, **result**, or **reason**. Then choose the linking expression and punctuation.

### A. Contrast: But / However

Both introduce a contrast, but their grammar differs.

| Pattern | Edited example |
| --- | --- |
| **Clause, but clause.** | I really want to learn new things, **but** I keep getting distracted by social media. |
| **Sentence. However, sentence.** | I would like to work in another city. **However,** I don't like the climate there. |
| **Clause; however, clause.** | I want to study; **however,** I find it difficult to concentrate. |

**But** is a coordinating conjunction. **However**, in this use, is a linking adverb; a comma alone cannot normally join two complete sentences with it.

- Avoid: I want to study, **however** I am tired.
- Use: I want to study. **However,** I am tired.
- Avoid using **but however** together to express the same contrast. This is a practical rule against redundancy, not a ban on the two words appearing anywhere in the same sentence.

**Useful expression:** I **can't help checking social media**. Here, **can't help + -ing** means being unable to resist doing something. Another option is **I can't stop myself from checking social media**.

### B. Addition: In Addition / Besides

| Expression | Edited example | Usage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **in addition** | English is useful for travel. **In addition,** it helps with study and work. | Adds information; neutral to formal. |
| **besides** | The house is affordable. **Besides,** it is spacious. | Adds another point, often another reason supporting an opinion. |
| **in addition to + noun / -ing** | **In addition to swimming,** I enjoy hiking. | **To** is a preposition here. |
| **besides + noun / -ing** | **Besides English,** I am interested in other languages. | Means “in addition to”; **besides** can also be a preposition. |

**Added distinction:** **Beside** means “next to”; **besides** means “in addition to” or introduces an extra point.

### C. Result: So / As a Result / Consequently / Thus

| Expression | Edited example |
| --- | --- |
| **so** | I woke up late, **so** I missed the bus. |
| **as a result** | I went to bed late last night. **As a result,** I didn't wake up early this morning. |
| **consequently** | I didn't get enough sleep. **Consequently,** I felt tired at work. |
| **thus** | The road was closed. **Thus,** we had to take another route. |

**So** is common in conversation. **Consequently** and **thus** sound more formal. Remember the article in **as a result**. When linking two complete sentences with the other three expressions, use a full stop or semicolon before the expression, as with **however**.

### D. Reason: Because / Because Of

- **Because + clause:** I didn't revise for the exam **because some guests came to visit**.
- **Because of + noun phrase:** I didn't revise for the exam **because of the visit**.
- **Because of + -ing phrase:** I was tired **because of working late**.

**Because** introduces a reason; it does not reverse the actual cause and effect. The reason can come first or second:

> **Because I went to bed late,** I didn't wake up early.  
> I didn't wake up early **because I went to bed late**.

**Added correction:** Avoid doubling the relationship in standard English: “Because I woke up late, so I missed the bus.” Use **because** or **so** for this connection.

## 2. Present Perfect: Form and Meaning

The present perfect connects a past event or situation with **the present**.

### A. Basic Structure — Added Reference

| Form | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Affirmative | **subject + have/has + past participle** | I **have visited** Harbin. |
| Negative | **subject + haven't/hasn't + past participle** | She **hasn't finished** her work. |
| Question | **Have/Has + subject + past participle?** | **Have** you **visited** Beijing? |
| Short answer | **Yes, subject + have/has.** / **No, subject + haven't/hasn't.** | Yes, I **have**. / No, I **haven't**. |

Use **has** with **he, she, it**; use **have** with **I, you, we, they**.

| Base verb | Simple past | Past participle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| visit | visited | **visited** |
| work | worked | **worked** |
| live | lived | **lived** |
| finish | finished | **finished** |
| be | was/were | **been** |
| go | went | **gone** |
| see | saw | **seen** |
| ride | rode | **ridden** |
| drive | drove | **driven** |
| break | broke | **broken** |

### B. Use 1: Life Experiences Up to Now

Use the present perfect for experiences when the focus is on **whether something has happened in your life up to now**, rather than when it happened.

> I **have visited** Beijing and Shanghai.  
> I **have visited** London **before**.  
> **Have** you **ever ridden** a motorcycle?  
> I **have never been** to London.

Compare a finished past event:

> I **visited** London **last year**.

**Added conversation pattern:** Start with an experience question, then switch to the simple past for details.

> A: **Have you ever visited** Harbin?  
> B: Yes, I **have**.  
> A: When **did you go**?  
> B: I **went** there last winter.

### C. Use 2: Completed Actions with Present Results

The action is complete, but its result matters now. The event is often recent, though **it does not have to have just happened**.

> I **have just finished** my work. I am free now.  
> Someone **has broken into** my house. The door is damaged, and some things are missing.  
> I **have lost** my keys. I can't get into my flat.

**Clarification:** “I have just finished my work” does not automatically mean the speaker is tired. Tiredness is one possible consequence; being available now is another. Context supplies the result.

**Added word order:** I have **just** finished. / I have **already** finished. / Have you finished **yet**? / I haven't finished **yet**.

### D. Use 3: Situations Continuing Until Now

Use the present perfect for a situation that started in the past and **still continues**.

> I **have lived in the city for five years**. *(I still live there.)*  
> I **have lived here since 2021**. *(An added grammar example.)*

| Expression | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **for + duration** | How long | for five years; for two hours; for a week |
| **since + starting point** | When it began | since 2021; since Monday; since I moved here |

Compare a finished period:

> I **lived in Shanghai for two years**, then moved away.

**For** does not automatically require the present perfect: it also works with the simple past when the period is over.

### E. Choose the Tense by Context — Added Clarification

The classroom shortcut “specific time = simple past” is useful only when it means **a finished past time locating the event**.

| Context | Natural choice | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Event at a finished past time | Simple past | I **finished** it **two hours ago**. |
| Experience up to now | Present perfect | I **have done** this **before**. |
| Situation beginning at a specific time and continuing now | Present perfect | I **have worked** here **since 2021**. |
| Unfinished period viewed up to now | Present perfect | I **have had** three meetings **so far today**. |
| A past event in an established past narrative, even without a time phrase | Simple past | I **opened** the door and **walked** inside. |

**No stated time does not automatically mean present perfect.** Likewise, **since 2021** includes a specific starting point but supports a connection to the present.

**Time expressions:** **Yesterday, last year, two hours ago**, and **just now** normally locate a finished event in the past. **Ever, never, before, already, just, yet, recently**, and **lately** can occur with the present perfect, but are not automatic tense rules. **Lately** is especially common for recent states or repeated activities; it is not simply a marker of life experience. American English also commonly uses the simple past with **just, already**, and **yet**.

### F. Present Perfect Continuous — Extension from the Reported Corrections

The supplied error analysis includes a related tense:

> It **has been raining for a week**.  
> I **have been working very hard lately**.

The structure is **have/has + been + -ing**. It often emphasises an ongoing or repeated activity over a period leading up to now, or one that has recently stopped with present effects.

**I have worked very hard lately** is also grammatical. **I have been working very hard lately** often sounds more natural when emphasising the recent ongoing effort. The reported classroom sentence “I have worked so hard these days” is understandable; **lately** is a more natural choice here.

## 3. Everyday Conversation

### A. Happiness, Stress & Volunteering

**Reported discussion:** the learner described a lack of rest and considerable work pressure. Riding a motorcycle gives her a sense of speed and freedom and helps her avoid traffic jams. She said the weather does not strongly affect her mood despite frequent local rain, and expressed the opinion that most people in her country are generally happy.

The discussion introduced expressions for wellbeing and positive attitudes. **Teach someone to swim** is a useful verb-pattern example.

**Model answer — added study material:**

> I **don't have enough time to relax**, and my job is **very stressful**. Riding my motorcycle makes me feel **free**. I also enjoy swimming and hiking. The weather doesn't usually **affect my mood** very much.

**Added vocabulary:** **Feel stressed**, **be under a lot of pressure**, **have a sense of freedom**, **get stuck in traffic**, **teach someone to swim**.

### B. Working Hours & School Life

Use **My working day runs from [time] to [time], including [duration] of breaks** when practising schedule descriptions. The total span of a day and the hours actually worked are different measures.

**Added past-tense example:** **When I was at school, we studied for several hours a day.**

### C. Hobbies, Local Sports & Evening Routines

- **Current hobbies:** Swimming and hiking. The learner reported many swimming pools in the city and hiking in the mountains on weekends when it does not rain.
- **Childhood:** Riding a bicycle to school and cycling in the mountains. The reported uphill journey took **two hours**, compared with **five minutes** downhill.
- **Sports mentioned:** Swimming, table tennis, basketball, football, volleyball, and kung fu. This is a classroom list, not a ranking of local popularity.
- **Evenings:** Returning home, taking a shower, calling relatives, and sometimes exercising or reading.
- **Social activities:** Describe spending time with friends without giving a regular location or schedule.

**Model answers — added study material:**

> There are **many swimming pools** in the city, so swimming is convenient.  
> If it **doesn't rain** at the weekend, I usually **go hiking**.  
> When I was a child, I **rode my bike to school**.  
> It **took me two hours to cycle uphill**, but only five minutes to come back down.  
> After work, I **take a shower** and **call my relatives**.  
> **Sometimes**, I meet friends and enjoy a quiet conversation.

### D. Weather: Express the Intended Comparison Carefully

The summary records the description that the city has “no winter” and temperatures stay above zero. **the city does have a winter season, and temperatures can fall below freezing.** Treat the original wording as an informal impression rather than a climate fact.

**Added alternative, if it matches the intended meaning:** **Winters here feel mild to me, but summers are hot.**

### A. Tense, Verb Forms & Adverbs

| Reported expression | Recommended expression | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “I didn't have enough time to rest.” | I **don't have** enough time to relax. | Use the present simple for a current situation. **Didn't have** is correct for a past situation. |
| “It have been raining for a week.” | It **has been raining** for a week. | **It + has**; this is present perfect continuous. |
| “I went to bed so lately... so I get up...” | I **went to bed late**, so I **didn't wake up early**. | **Late** means after the expected time; **lately** means recently. Use past forms for these completed events. |
| “I didn't awake early.” | I **didn't wake up early**. | **Awake** can be a verb, so the source wording is not necessarily ungrammatical; **wake up** is more usual in everyday speech. |
| “I have worked so hardly these days.” | I **have been working so hard lately**. | **Hard** means with effort; **hardly** means almost not. The continuous form is a natural edited alternative. |
| “I have visit Harbin.” | I **have visited** Harbin. | Use a **past participle** after have/has. |
| “When I'm in high school? We learned twelve hours one day.” | **When I was in high school, we studied twelve hours a day.** | **Was** fits the past period; **study** describes the activity; **a day** expresses a daily amount. |

**Added contrast pairs:**

- I work **hard**. = I put in a lot of effort.
- I **hardly work**. = I do almost no work.
- I went to bed **late**. = I went to bed after the usual or desired time.
- I have been tired **lately**. = I have been tired recently.

### B. Vocabulary, Agreement & Verb Patterns

| Reported expression | Recommended expression | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “The work is under much pressure.” | **My work is very stressful.** / **I'm under a lot of pressure at work.** | Describe the work or the person experiencing pressure. |
| “You're very stressful.” | **My work is very stressful.** / **I feel very stressed.** | **Stressful** describes a cause of stress; **stressed** describes the feeling. The original can suggest that the person causes stress, which was not the intended meaning. |
| “I like driving a motorcycle.” | I like **riding a motorcycle**. | Learn the vehicle collocation; the supplied timestamp is a broad span. |
| “There are many swimming pool.” | There are **many swimming pools**. | **Many + plural countable noun**. |
| “Swimming is a very popular sports here.” | Swimming is **a very popular sport** here. | **A** requires a singular countable noun. This is not a rule that every complement must match its subject in number. |
| “We teach blind people swim.” | We **teach blind people to swim**. | **Teach + someone + to + verb**. Teach is not a bare-infinitive causative like make or let. |
| “They are more happy than me.” | They are **happier than me**. | **Happier** is the usual comparative here. **Than I am** is another correct ending. |

**Transport collocations — added reference:**

| Verb | Common combinations |
| --- | --- |
| **ride** | ride a bike; ride a motorcycle; ride a horse |
| **drive** | drive a car; drive a bus; drive a truck |
| **take** | take a bus; take a train; take a taxi |

**Do not reduce this to wheel count.** You can **ride in a car** as a passenger, and **ride a bus** is common in American English. **Drive** usually means controlling a motor vehicle; **ride** has several uses depending on the vehicle and context.

**Added verb-pattern comparison:** They **teach me to swim**. / They **make me practise**. / They **let me try**.

| Reported confusion | Review |

## 5. Language Comparison

**Reported discussion:** the learner described Chinese grammar as relatively simple because verbs do not change for tense, while Chinese writing is difficult. She also mentioned grammatical gender in Russian. The teacher discussed gender in Arabic and briefly practised basic Chinese counting from one to five.

**Added clarification:** Mandarin verbs do not inflect for tense as English verbs do, but Mandarin still expresses time and aspect through context, time expressions, and particles. This does not mean its grammar has no complexity. Russian has **masculine, feminine, and neuter** grammatical gender; Arabic generally has **masculine and feminine**.

The summary labels the Arabic words for chair as feminine and table as masculine. This should not be memorised as a reliable example: common standard Arabic **kursī** (“chair”) is masculine, while **ṭāwila** (“table”) is feminine. The exact words or dialect used in class are unavailable; grammatical gender belongs to a word, not to the object itself.

**Practical implication:** When speaking English, check **when the situation happens**, **whether the time period is finished**, and **whether the situation continues now**, then choose the verb form.

## 7. Review Exercises — Added Study Material

### A. Choose the Verb Form

1. I ______ Harbin last winter. *(visit)*
2. I ______ Harbin twice in my life so far. *(visit)*
3. She ______ here since 2021 and still lives here. *(live)*
4. I ______ in Shanghai for two years before moving away. *(live)*
5. It ______ for a week, and it is still raining. *(rain; use the continuous form)*
6. When I ______ in high school, I studied twelve hours a day. *(be)*
7. ______ you ever ______ a motorcycle? *(ride)*
8. I ______ enough time to relax these days. *(not have; describe a current situation)*

**Answers:** 1. **visited**; 2. **have visited**; 3. **has lived**; 4. **lived**; 5. **has been raining**; 6. **was**; 7. **Have … ridden**; 8. **don't have**.

### B. Correct the Errors

1. I have visit Beijing.
2. My job is stressed, and I feel stressful.
3. I work hardly because I want to improve.
4. I went to bed lately last night.
5. There are many swimming pool near my home.
6. We teach people swim.
7. I stayed at home because of it was raining.
8. I want to study, however I keep checking my phone.

**Suggested answers:**

1. I **have visited** Beijing.
2. My job is **stressful**, and I feel **stressed**.
3. I work **hard** because I want to improve.
4. I went to bed **late** last night.
5. There are **many swimming pools** near my home.
6. We teach people **to swim**.
7. I stayed at home **because it was raining**. / I stayed at home **because of the rain**.
8. I want to study. **However,** I keep checking my phone. / I want to study, **but** I keep checking my phone.

### C. Connect the Ideas

Rewrite each pair using the expression in brackets.

1. English helps me travel. It helps me at work. *(in addition)*
2. I woke up late. I missed the bus. *(as a result)*
3. The house is affordable. It is far from work. *(but)*
4. I didn't go hiking. It rained. *(because)*

**Suggested answers:**

1. English helps me travel. **In addition, it helps me at work.**
2. I woke up late. **As a result, I missed the bus.**
3. The house is affordable, **but it is far from work**.
4. I didn't go hiking **because it rained**.

### D. Explain the Meaning Difference

1. **I have lived here for five years.** / **I lived there for five years.**
2. **I work hard.** / **I hardly work.**
3. **I don't have time to rest.** / **I didn't have time to rest.**

**Answers:** 1. The first normally means I still live here; the second describes a finished period there. 2. A lot of effort versus almost no work. 3. A current situation versus a past situation.
