## 1. A Favourite Sport: Swimming

### Reported content

- The learner chose **swimming** as a favourite sport and described going with her friend or colleagues.
- She learned from a coach at a nearby gym while she was in high school.
- At first, she was afraid of the water and thought swimming was difficult.
- She associated its popularity in southern China with the region's rivers and lakes. This is a **reported observation**, not a verified explanation of participation rates.
- Swimming helps her concentrate because she cannot keep checking her phone or watching videos while swimming.
- She prefers the feeling of exercising in water to feeling hot and sweaty during some other activities.

**Clarification:** People **can sweat while swimming**. A more accurate personal claim is **“I feel cooler and less uncomfortable in the water”**, rather than “Swimming does not make people sweat.” The concentration benefit is her experience, not a rule that running is necessarily distracting.

### Added cue card and structure

Describe a sport you enjoy. Say **what it is**, **when and how you learned it**, **who you do it with**, and explain **why you enjoy it**.

**Structure:** Identify → Learning background → Present routine → Benefits → Personal evaluation.

### Added model answer

> One of my favourite sports is swimming. I usually go with my friend or some of my colleagues, and it's something I've continued to enjoy since I learned it in high school.
>
> I took lessons with a coach at a nearby gym. At first, I was afraid of the water, so I thought it would be quite difficult. As I became more comfortable, though, I started to enjoy the experience much more.
>
> What I particularly like about swimming is that it helps me concentrate on what I'm doing. When I'm doing some other activities, it's easy to become distracted by my phone. While I'm swimming, I can't keep checking messages or watching videos, so I get a proper break from those distractions.
>
> I also like the feeling of being in the water. I don't enjoy feeling hot and sticky when I exercise, and swimming feels more comfortable to me, even when I'm working hard.
>
> Going with people I know makes it enjoyable as well. For me, it's a way to stay active while taking my attention away from work and everyday worries.

**Model note:** The answer uses the reported facts with added connecting language and reflection. Adjust any personal effect or preference that does not match your experience.

## 2. Vocabulary Distinctions from the Discussion

### Listen to and Hear

| Expression | Main meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **listen to** | Direct your attention to a sound or speaker. | **I listened to the coach's instructions.** |
| **hear** | Perceive a sound or receive information. | **I heard a noise outside. / I can hear you clearly.** |

Use **listen to + the source of the sound**: **listen to music / listen to the teacher**. **Listen!** is also a complete command when no object follows.

The distinction is about **attention versus perception**, not an absolute rule that hearing is accidental. **“Did you hear the interview?”** can describe an interview someone deliberately listened to. **Heard** is the past form of **hear**; **listened** is the past form of **listen**.

### Memorise, Remember, Memory, and Memories

| Word | Use | Added example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **memorise / memorize** | Verb: deliberately learn something so that you can recall it. | **I memorised the new words.** |
| **remember** | Verb: retain or bring something back to mind. | **I remember my first swimming lesson.** |
| **memory** | Noun: the ability to remember, or one remembered experience. | **She has a good memory. / That's a happy memory.** |
| **memories** | Plural noun: remembered experiences. | **The visit brought back memories of school.** |

**Pronunciation targets:** **memorise /ˈmeməraɪz/**, **memory /ˈmeməri/**, **memories /ˈmeməriz/**. These are reference forms for practice; the supplied notes do not establish the student's exact pronunciation error.

**Meaning matters:** You **memorise a speech** deliberately; you **remember a former teacher**. Memorising is not simply a substitute for every use of remembering.

### Approach and Go Up To

- **Approach + person or thing:** **I approached him. / We approached the building.** No **to** is needed after the verb **approach** in these examples.
- **Go up to + person or thing:** **I went up to the teacher. / I went up to the window.**

**Correction to the summary:** **Go up to** can refer to objects and places as well as people. It is not restricted to approaching a person.

### See, Meet, Recognise, and Remember

| Word or phrase | Useful meaning in this story | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **see** | Notice visually, or visit/spend time with someone, depending on context. | **I saw him across the room. / I'm seeing a friend tomorrow.** |
| **meet** | Come together with someone, either by arrangement or by chance. | **We arranged to meet. / I met an old classmate by chance.** |
| **run into / bump into** | Encounter someone unexpectedly. | **I ran into my old teacher.** |
| **recognise / recognize** | Identify someone or something as familiar. | **He didn't recognise me at first.** |
| **remember** | Recall a person, event, fact, or detail. | **He remembered teaching our class.** |

**Correction to the summary:** **See = accidental; meet = intentional** is not a reliable distinction. State the circumstances with **by chance**, **unexpectedly**, **by arrangement**, or **without letting him know beforehand**.

**Useful contrast:** **I recognised his face, but I couldn't remember his name.**

## 3. An Embarrassing Experience: Visiting a Former Teacher

### Anonymised Practice Scenario

- The speaker visits a former teacher after a long period without contact.
- The visit was not arranged in advance, and the teacher does not immediately recognise the visitor.
- A conversation helps them reconnect.
- The speaker reflects on why it is considerate to contact people beforehand.

Locations, dates, institutions, origins and private circumstances have been omitted.

### Added cue card and structure

Describe an embarrassing experience. Say **when and where it happened**, **who was involved**, **what happened**, and explain **how you felt and what you learned**.

**Structure:** Earlier relationship → Later visit → Unexpected reaction → Conversation → Reflection.

### Added model answer

> An embarrassing experience I remember involved a former English teacher. He taught me some years earlier, and I remembered our lessons fondly.
>
> Several years later, I went to see him at an educational institution. The problem was that I hadn't contacted him beforehand. I expected him to remember me, but when I approached him, he didn't recognise me at first.
>
> I felt embarrassed because I had assumed that the connection would be immediately obvious to him. We then talked about our school days and two other students, and that helped him remember the time we had spent together.
>
> Looking back, I should have considered the situation from his point of view. A lot of time had passed, and he wasn't expecting a visit from a former student. I could have sent a message first, briefly explained who I was and checked whether it was a convenient time.
>
> The experience reminded me that a friendly intention doesn't always make an unexpected visit easy for the other person. If I wanted to reconnect with someone after a long time now, I'd contact them in advance.

**Model note:** The reflective wording is added practice material. The location and events are reported from the summary, not independently verified; the disputed year and unclear job title are omitted.

## 4. Past Simple and Past Perfect: Make the Sequence Clear

**Past simple** gives the main events or states in a finished past situation. **Past perfect — had + past participle —** looks back to something earlier than another past reference point.

| Part of the story | Example | Why this tense fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| School background | **He taught me English when I was in high school.** | A finished past situation. |
| Later visit | **I went to his laboratory several years later.** | The main event in the later story. |
| Missing preparation before the visit | **I hadn't contacted him beforehand.** | Looks back from the visit to the earlier period. |
| Reaction during the visit | **He didn't recognise me at first.** | What happened at that point in the story. |
| Reflection now | **I now think it's better to contact people in advance.** | A present belief resulting from a past experience. |

### Both of These Can Be Correct

> **I didn't call him before I went to the university.**

> **I hadn't called him before I went to the university.**

**Before** already makes the order clear, so past simple is not automatically an error. Past perfect makes the earlier situation more explicit from the viewpoint of the later event.

An especially natural example is:

> **When I arrived at the laboratory, I realised I hadn't called him beforehand.**

**Tense consistency does not mean using one tense throughout.** Present preferences, past events, and earlier past events can all appear in one answer when the time relationships are clear. Past perfect can describe earlier states as well as actions; the key distinction is time, not simply “state versus action.”

## 5. Reported Grammar Issues and Precise Corrections

| Reported issue | Corrected or improved expression | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “It doesn't let me concentrated.” | **It doesn't let me concentrate.** | **Let + object + base verb**. For the positive effect of swimming: **Swimming helps me concentrate.** |
| “one of my favourite exercise” | **one of my favourite exercises** | **One of + plural countable noun**. For the topic itself: **Swimming is one of my favourite sports.** |
| “listen music” | **listen to music** | Include **to** before the sound source. |
| “I enjoy talking… and spend time…” | **I enjoy talking…, spending time…, and exercising.** | Actions sharing the complement position after **enjoy** should use the **-ing** form. |
| “The situation was occurred.” | **The incident occurred. / It happened.** | **Occur** and **happen** are intransitive in this use and do not take an ordinary passive form. |
| “I didn't call him before I went…” | **Grammatical as written**, with a suitable ending. **I hadn't called him…** is also possible. | Past perfect is a useful choice, not a compulsory replacement whenever two past events appear. |
| “made me embarrassment” | **It made me feel embarrassed. / It caused me embarrassment.** | Choose a structure that fits the adjective **embarrassed** or noun **embarrassment**. |
| Using **memory** where a verb is needed | **I remember… / I memorised…**, depending on meaning. | **Memory/memories** are nouns; remembering and deliberately memorising are different actions. |

### Other and Another: More Than a Singular/Plural Shortcut

- **Other + plural noun:** **other sports / other people**.
- **Other + uncountable noun:** **other information / other advice**.
- **Another + singular countable noun:** **another sport / another person**.
- A singular countable noun can follow **other** when another determiner is present: **the other student / my other friend / one other reason**.
- **Another + number + plural noun** is also possible for an additional amount: **another two days**.

### Parallel Structure Depends on the Sentence

> **I enjoy swimming, walking, and spending time outdoors.**

All three activities depend on **enjoy**, so they use **-ing**.

> **I enjoy swimming, and I spend time outdoors every weekend.**

This is also correct: it contains two coordinated clauses, each with its own subject and verb. Do not change every later verb to **-ing** merely because an earlier word ends in **-ing**.

### Embarrassed, Embarrassing, and Embarrassment

- **I felt embarrassed.** — my feeling.
- **It was an embarrassing situation.** — the situation caused that feeling.
- **The misunderstanding caused me embarrassment.** — the noun in a natural structure.

**“I felt total embarrassment”** is grammatically possible, but **“I felt really embarrassed”** is usually more natural in an everyday spoken story.

## 6. Learning Strengths and Review Priorities

**Reported strength:** The supplied analysis notes self-correction, including a change from **become** to **became**. This suggests useful monitoring of tense while speaking, although the recording is unavailable for assessing frequency or overall fluency.

**Suggested priorities:**

1. Check short verb patterns: **let me concentrate; enjoy swimming; listen to instructions**.
2. Practise **one of + plural noun** and accurate uses of **other/another**.
3. Plan a story with three time markers: **when I was at school → several years later → looking back now**.
4. Use past perfect when it helps the listener understand an earlier event; do not force it into every sentence.
5. Make a brief correction when needed, then continue the idea rather than restarting the whole answer.
