## 1. A Close Friend

### Reported content

The learner described a close relationship with her friend, shared interests such as travel, conversations they used to have, and the support and security associated with their relationship.

### Grammar and natural expressions

| Reported wording | Correction or improvement | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “I go on well with my friend.” | **I get on well with my friend.** | **Get on well with** or **get along well with** = have a good relationship. |
| “She is on outgoing girl.” | **She is an outgoing person.** | Use **an** before a vowel sound. **Woman/person** may suit an adult better than **girl**. |
| “very similar with me” | **very similar to me** | **Similar to**, but **have things in common with**. |
| “I love travels too.” | **I love travelling too.** / **I love to travel too.** | **Love + -ing** or **love + to-infinitive**. **Travelling/traveling** are British/American spellings. |
| “That's the reason why… I went to…” | **That's why I went to…** | Use the past simple for completed trips. |
| “why was young…” | **When I was younger…** | **When** introduces a time; **why** asks for a reason. |
| “female topics” / “female discuss” | **the things we used to discuss** | Name the actual topics when possible. **Women's issues** has a more specific social meaning. |
| “we were so female” | **We are both women.** | Likely intended meaning; the original fragment is unclear. Use the present for a current description. |

**Useful phrases:** **have a lot in common**, **support each other**, **feel safe around someone**, **turn to someone for advice**, **as I've grown older**.

### Used to versus still

- **We used to discuss school every evening.** A past habit that is no longer the same.
- **We still discuss our plans regularly.** An ongoing habit.
- **We have always been close.** A relationship extending from the past to now.

Do not choose **used to** simply because something happened in the past: it normally suggests that the habit or situation has changed.

### Added model answer

> I'd like to talk about my friend. We get on really well and have a lot in common, especially our interest in travelling. When we were younger, we used to talk about school and everyday life. As I've grown older, our conversations have changed, but we are still close. I feel safe talking to her because I know she will listen. What matters most to me is that we support each other.

**Question distinction:** **What is she like?** asks about personality: “She's outgoing and caring.” **What does she like?** asks about preferences: “She likes travelling.”

## 2. A Language You Have Learned

### Reported content

The learner discussed learning Russian at university, opportunities to learn from Russian speakers, textbooks, tests, poems, pronunciation difficulties, and continuing to practise afterwards. Language-learning experiences were compared in general terms.

| Reported wording | Correction or improvement | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Three foreigner languages” | **three foreign languages** | **Foreign** is an adjective; **foreigner** is a person. |
| “I started a learning Russian.” | **I started learning Russian.** | Also **started to learn**; no **a** before the verb form. |
| “many peoples in my university” | **many people at my university** | **People** is the normal plural of **person**. **Peoples** means distinct ethnic or national populations. |
| “Russian foreigners… teach language” | **Russian teachers came to teach the language.** | Use **teachers**, **Russian speakers**, or **native speakers of Russian** if supported by context. **Expats** adds an unconfirmed residence status. |
| “I have a environment…” | **I had a supportive environment for learning Russian.** | Past experience → **had**; **an environment**, but **a supportive environment**. |
| “the textbooks is… it's…” | **The textbooks were not very thick.** | Plural subject → **were**; plural pronoun → **they**. |
| “a vowel… that is not exist” | **a vowel sound that does not exist in my first language** | **Exist** is a verb: **does not exist**, without **is**. The language comparison needs context. |
| “this voice is exist in Arabic” | **This sound exists in Arabic.** | **Sound** describes a speech sound; **voice** describes someone's vocal sound. |
| “relax your toe” | **relax your tongue** | **Tongue** is the speech organ; **toe** is part of the foot. |
| “verbatim sound” | **a trill** / **a trilled r** | **Verbatim** means using exactly the original words. |
| “we have have a test” | **We had a test.** | Past simple for a completed test. |
| “a lot of poem” | **a lot of poems** | **Poem** is countable; **poetry** is uncountable. |
| “I back to the North China.” | **I went back to northern China.** / **I returned to North China.** | **Back** needs a movement verb in this meaning. |
| “played happy” | **We had a great time.** | Learn the whole collocation. |

### Added pronunciation clarification

The Russian **trilled r /r/** is a **consonant**, not a vowel. The tongue tip vibrates near the ridge behind the upper front teeth. Russian vowel difficulties are a separate issue.

Spanish and many varieties of Arabic also have an alveolar r sound, though its realization varies. This comparison can help identify the sound; it does not mean all three languages pronounce every r identically.

**Back can be a verb in other meanings:** **I back your proposal** means “I support it.” In **go back home**, it is an adverb. Therefore, the classroom rule should be **“back cannot replace go/return in this sentence.”**

### Added model answer

> One language I studied was Russian. I started learning it at university, where I had opportunities to learn from Russian speakers. Pronunciation was one of the hardest parts, especially the trilled r. We also studied poems and took tests. The experience showed me that learning a language involves both regular practice and an interest in its culture.

## 3. Someone You Respect

### Reported content

The learner described a former teacher who was reportedly still teaching and had worked for many years. Important qualities included passion, hard work, and a serious approach to teaching.

| Reported wording | Correction or improvement | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “The person who I am respected” | **The person I respect** | **I respect him** is active; **I am respected** means other people respect me. |
| “he was my teacher… he still teach” | **He was my teacher, and he still teaches.** | Different times justify different tenses. **He teaches** needs **-es**. |
| “insist in this teaching” | **He has remained dedicated to teaching.** | **Insist on** = demand or maintain a position; **persist in** = continue despite difficulty. |
| “for almost a third years” | **for many years** | **Third** = ordinal number; **thirty** = 30. |
| “passion on… his class” | **a passion for teaching** | **Passion for + noun/-ing**. |
| “Raise the Respecting…” | **I really respect him.** | The original is unclear; a short sentence conveys the likely meaning. |
| “give courses” | **teach courses** / **teach classes** | **Give lessons** is also natural. **Take classes** usually means attend as a student. |
| “characterized by serenity” | **He takes his work seriously.** | **Serenity** means calmness; **seriousness** concerns a serious attitude. Both are valid words with different meanings. |

**Former versus ex-:** **My former teacher** means he taught me previously; he may still teach other students. **Ex-** also applies to jobs and roles, as in **ex-president** and **ex-colleague**, not only relationships. **Former** often sounds more neutral or formal.

**Optional formal expression:** **He is characterized by passion, hard work, and dedication.** For speaking, **He is passionate, hardworking, and dedicated** is simpler and more natural. **He takes teaching seriously** avoids implying that he is unfriendly.

### Present perfect continuous versus past perfect continuous

| Sentence | Reference point | What it tells us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **He has been teaching for many years.** | Now | His teaching began in the past and continues now, or has ended very recently. Here, the summary confirms he still teaches. |
| **When I met him, he had been teaching for twenty years.** | A past meeting | Twenty years of teaching preceded that past point. He may have continued afterwards. |
| **He taught for thirty years before retiring.** | A completed career | The added word **retiring** makes the ending explicit. |

**Had been working does not automatically mean someone has stopped working.** It places an ongoing activity before a past reference point. **Has been working** is specifically the **present perfect continuous**, not merely the present perfect simple.

The reported “Halloween” fragment is too unclear to diagnose confidently. Learn the tense name directly: **present perfect continuous = has/have been + -ing**.

### Added model answer

> Someone I really respect is my former teacher. Although he no longer teaches me, he still works as a teacher and has been teaching for many years. He is passionate about his subject and takes his work seriously. I admire his dedication because maintaining that level of commitment over many years is difficult. He is a good example of someone who cares deeply about his work.

## 4. A Place You Recently Visited

### Reported content

The learner described a trip to a volcanic area, a reported temperature of around **−40°C**, only three tourists, concerns about staff availability and safety, nervousness, and a positive outcome. The precise location, closure status, and staff circumstances are not established by the supplied fragments.

| Reported wording | Correction or improvement | Learning point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “In which have many volcanoes” | **a region which has many volcanoes** / **a region where there are many volcanoes** | **Which** acts as a subject; **where** introduces a location clause. |
| “temperature is minus forty degree” | **The temperature was minus forty degrees Celsius.** | Past **was**; plural **degrees** for −40. **It was extremely cold** adds emphasis. |
| “There was only three tourists.” | **There were only three tourists.** | **Three tourists** takes **were**. |
| “climb that mountain…” | **climb that volcano** | More specific if referring to the same volcano. **Mountain** is not necessarily wrong. |
| “no official… blue worker” | **There were no staff members there.** | Likely intended meaning; **blue worker** is unclear. **Blue-collar worker** has a different meaning. |
| “volcano will explode… low property” | **The probability of an eruption was low.** | **Probability** = likelihood; **property** = possession or characteristic. Volcanoes **erupt**. This is a reported belief, not verified risk information. |
| “it's closed in that day” | **It was closed that day.** | For an actual closure. **It would close later that day** means a future closure viewed from the past; **it was closing** means closing was in progress or arranged. |
| “we a flat very nervous” | **We felt very nervous.** | **Felt** is the past of **feel**. |
| “lose the temperature” | **lose body heat** / **develop hypothermia** | **Hypothermia** is a medical condition, not simply feeling cold. Use **we were worried about hypothermia** if it did not actually occur. |
| “working stuff were on holiday” | **The staff members were on holiday.** | **Staff** = employees; **stuff** = things/material. **Staff members** gives clear plural agreement. |

### Staff, stuff, person, and people

- **One person → three people** in ordinary conversation. **Persons** occurs in formal or legal contexts.
- **Staff** is a collective noun. **The staff is…** is common in American English when treated as a unit; **the staff are…** is common in British English when referring to the people. It is not always singular.
- **A staff member / three staff members** avoids ambiguity.
- **Stuff** is usually uncountable: **There was some stuff in the room.**

### Added storytelling framework: Setting → Challenge → Reaction → Outcome

**Added model answer based on reported details:**

> I'd like to describe a trip to a volcanic area. It was extremely cold—around minus forty degrees Celsius—and there were only three tourists there. That made the place feel unusually quiet. I felt nervous about the conditions, especially the cold. Luckily, everything went well. The trip was memorable because the setting was so different from places I normally visit.

**Outcome markers:** **In the end** introduces the final result; **luckily/fortunately** evaluates a fortunate result. They can be combined: **In the end, everything went well. Luckily, nobody was hurt.** The second sentence is an added language example, not a confirmed detail of the trip.

## 5. Speaking More Clearly

Use **Well**, **Let me think**, or **To be honest** briefly when needed. **To be honest** introduces a candid opinion; it is not appropriate as a filler in every sentence. **Discourse markers** also organize ideas, so they are broader than hesitation fillers.

For each answer, prepare **four keywords**, one for each step:

| Topic | Four-step plan |
| --- | --- |
| Family | **Person → Shared interests → Support → Importance** |
| Language | **Language → Learning context → Difficulty → Reflection** |
| Respected person | **Person → Relationship → Qualities → Evidence** |
| Travel | **Setting → Challenge → Reaction → Outcome** |

**Simple grammar with clear evidence is more useful than a complicated sentence you cannot finish.** Add one concrete example after each important claim.
