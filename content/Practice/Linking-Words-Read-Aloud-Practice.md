# Linking Words — Read-Aloud Practice

**Privacy note:** Personal and third-party details have been removed or generalised. First-person stories and altered quotations are anonymised study examples, not a biographical record or verbatim transcript.

**Purpose:** Understand and practise every expression in the supplied “Linking Words in English” image.  
**Source:** [Original chart](../Resources/LinkingWordsInEnglish.jpg). All example sentences, usage explanations, and practice passages below are **added study material**, not quotations from the chart. Examples are illustrative, not claims about the learner's life.

Read each pair aloud twice. Then replace one detail with something from your own life. You can cover all twelve groups tonight in three rounds: **1–4**, **5–8**, and **9–12**, with a short break between rounds. Understanding one natural sentence is more useful than memorising a connector alone.

## Before You Read: Four Useful Patterns

- **Because + subject + verb:** I stayed home **because it was raining**.
- **Because of / due to / despite / in spite of + noun or -ing:** I stayed home **because of the rain**. I went out **despite feeling tired**.
- **Although / even though / if / unless + subject + verb:** **Although I was tired**, I practised English.
- **However / therefore / moreover** can connect ideas, but do not join two complete sentences with only a comma: **I was tired. However, I kept practising.** Also correct: **I was tired; however, I kept practising.**

The chart mixes connectors with ordinary vocabulary and incomplete phrases. The notes below identify these cases rather than treating every item as interchangeable.

## 1. Emphasis — Making a Point Stronger

### Undoubtedly

Meaning: almost certainly; without doubt. Fairly formal and strong.

- **Undoubtedly,** regular practice improves speaking skills.
- She is **undoubtedly** one of the best teachers I have had.

### Indeed

Use to confirm or strengthen an idea; more common in careful speech or writing.

- Learning a language takes time. **Indeed,** it can take years to become fluent.
- “Was the lesson useful?” “It was **indeed**.”

### Obviously

Use when something seems clear. It can sound dismissive if the listener does not find it obvious.

- **Obviously,** we need warm clothes if it is snowing.
- She was **obviously** pleased with the good news.

### Particularly / In particular

Use to highlight one thing within a group.

- I **particularly** enjoy talking about travel.
- I enjoy learning languages. Russian, **in particular,** interests me.

### Especially

- I enjoy outdoor activities, **especially** hiking.
- My friend is **especially** good at making people feel comfortable.

### Clearly

- **Clearly,** we need more time to finish this exercise.
- He **clearly** cares about his students.

### Importantly

- **Importantly,** you do not need difficult words to express a good idea.
- The hotel is affordable. **More importantly,** it is close to the station.

### Absolutely

Often strengthens certain adjectives or expresses strong agreement; not a general sentence connector.

- The view from the mountain was **absolutely beautiful**.
- “Should we respect other people's privacy?” “**Absolutely.**”

### Definitely

- I will **definitely** review these expressions tomorrow.
- This exercise is **definitely** easier the second time.

### Without a doubt

- **Without a doubt,** my friend is someone I can rely on.
- This was, **without a doubt,** the coldest trip I have ever taken.

### Never

**Never** means “not at any time.” It is a negative adverb, not a linking word.

- I have **never** visited Morocco.
- I **never** check my phone while driving.

### It should be noted

Usually **It should be noted that + clause**. Formal; in conversation, try **Remember that…**

- **It should be noted that** these examples are for practice.
- **It should be noted that** some expressions are more common in writing.

## 2. Addition — Adding Another Point

### Additionally / An additional…

**Additionally** introduces a point. **An additional** needs a noun.

- The course includes speaking practice. **Additionally,** it includes grammar review.
- **An additional benefit** is that I feel more confident.

### Furthermore

Formal; useful for a developed argument.

- Public transport reduces traffic. **Furthermore,** it can lower emissions per passenger.
- The apartment is spacious. **Furthermore,** it is close to my office.

### Also

Usually before a main verb, but after **be**.

- My friend likes travelling. I **also like** travelling.
- The teacher is patient. He **is also** encouraging.

### Too

Usually at the end in this meaning.

- I like travelling **too**.
- My friend will be there, and her partner will come **too**.

### As well as that

- The room is bright. **As well as that,** it has a lovely view.
- Reading teaches me vocabulary. **As well as that,** it gives me ideas to discuss.

### Along with

Follow with a noun or -ing form, not an independent sentence.

- I packed a jacket **along with** some warm gloves.
- **Along with learning new words,** I practise using them in sentences.

### Besides

As a connector, it adds another supporting reason. It can also mean “in addition to.”

- I don't want to eat out. **Besides,** we already have food at home.
- **Besides English,** she studies French.

### In addition

- I read aloud every evening. **In addition,** I record my voice.
- **In addition to reading aloud,** I practise speaking without notes.

### Moreover

Fairly formal, like **furthermore**.

- The plan is practical. **Moreover,** it is affordable.
- The museum preserves local history. **Moreover,** it offers activities for children.

### Not only… but also

Keep the two parts grammatically parallel.

- She is **not only kind but also patient**.
- Learning English helps me **not only at work but also when I travel**.

### In addition to this

- The lessons improve my grammar. **In addition to this,** they build my confidence.
- The town has beautiful scenery. **In addition to this,** it has excellent food.

### Apart from this

In these examples, it adds another point. In other contexts, **apart from** can mean “except for.”

- The hotel is comfortable. **Apart from this,** it is reasonably priced.
- The app helps me learn vocabulary. **Apart from this,** it tracks my progress.

## 3. Contrast — Showing a Difference

### Unlike

**Unlike + noun/pronoun**.

- **Unlike me,** my friend enjoys cooking.
- **Unlike the first textbook,** the second one has audio exercises.

### Nevertheless

Fairly formal: despite what was just mentioned.

- The exercise was difficult. **Nevertheless,** I finished it.
- It was extremely cold. **Nevertheless,** the scenery was beautiful.

### On the other hand

Use to present another side of the same issue.

- Fame can bring opportunities. **On the other hand,** it can reduce privacy.
- Living alone gives me freedom. **On the other hand,** it can feel lonely.

### Nonetheless

Similar to **nevertheless**; fairly formal.

- I made several mistakes. **Nonetheless,** my meaning was clear.
- The journey was long. **Nonetheless,** it was worth it.

### Despite / In spite of

Use **noun/-ing**, or **the fact that + clause**. Never **despite of**.

- **Despite the cold,** we enjoyed the trip.
- **In spite of feeling nervous,** I answered the question.

### In contrast (to)

- My hometown is quiet. **In contrast,** Beijing feels very busy.
- **In contrast to my first attempt,** my second answer was well organised.

### While

Can express contrast or time. These examples express contrast.

- I prefer tea, **while** my friend prefers coffee.
- **While** online lessons are convenient, face-to-face lessons can feel more personal.

### Whereas

- My friend is outgoing, **whereas** I am more reserved.
- This exercise focuses on accuracy, **whereas** the next one focuses on fluency.

### Alternatively

Introduces another option rather than a direct contradiction.

- We could eat out. **Alternatively,** we could cook at home.
- You can write your answer. **Alternatively,** you can record it.

### Conversely

Means an opposite or reversed relationship. More precise and formal than **however**.

- Higher prices can reduce demand. **Conversely,** lower prices can increase it.
- Good sleep can improve concentration. **Conversely,** poor sleep can weaken it.

### Even so

- I was tired. **Even so,** I practised for ten minutes.
- The restaurant was expensive. **Even so,** I would go there again.

### Differing from

This is a participle phrase, not a convenient general connector. **Different from** or **unlike** is often easier.

- **Differing from the first plan in cost,** the second plan is cheaper.
- This method **differs from** the one I used before.

## 4. Order — Organising Points and Events

### First / Firstly

- **First,** I will describe my friend.
- **Firstly,** we need to understand the question.

### Second / Secondly

- **Second,** I will explain what we have in common.
- **Secondly,** we should choose one clear example.

### Third / Thirdly

- **Third,** I will explain why she matters to me.
- **Thirdly,** we need to check our verb forms.

### Finally

Can introduce a last point or a long-awaited event.

- **Finally,** I will describe how the trip ended.
- After several attempts, I **finally** pronounced the word correctly.

### At this time

Means “at the time being discussed” or “currently”; it does not simply mean “next.”

- The shop opens at nine. **At this time,** the street is usually quiet.
- We are not accepting new applications **at this time**.

### Following

Usually **following + noun**, meaning “after.” For a sentence connector, use **afterwards**.

- **Following the lesson,** I reviewed my notes.
- **Following our discussion,** I changed my study plan.

### Previously

- I now study every day. **Previously,** I studied only at weekends.
- I had **previously** visited Beijing for work.

### Before

- **Before the lesson,** I read my notes.
- **Before I answer,** let me think for a moment.

### Subsequently

Formal: later, after that.

- She completed the course and **subsequently** found a new job.
- The museum closed for repairs. It **subsequently** reopened.

### Above all

Expresses priority, not chronological order.

- **Above all,** I want to communicate clearly.
- When travelling in winter, stay warm and, **above all,** stay safe.

### Last but not least

Introduces a final point that still matters.

- **Last but not least,** remember to enjoy the learning process.
- **Last but not least,** I want to thank my teacher.

### First and foremost

Introduces the most important priority.

- **First and foremost,** a teacher should care about students.
- **First and foremost,** I want to feel confident when speaking.

## 5. Result — Explaining What Happened Because of Something

### As a result

- I practised every day. **As a result,** I became more fluent.
- It snowed heavily. **As a result,** our flight was cancelled.

### As a consequence (of)

Without **of**, introduce a result. With **of**, introduce the cause as a noun phrase.

- I stayed up too late. **As a consequence,** I felt tired the next morning.
- **As a consequence of the storm,** several roads were closed.

### Therefore

- The road is closed. **Therefore,** we need another route.
- The examples are short. **Therefore,** they are easier to remember.

### Thus

Formal; often expresses a logical result.

- The two answers have the same meaning. **Thus,** either one is acceptable.
- The app saves my notes, **thus making** revision easier.

### Consequently

- The temperature dropped sharply. **Consequently,** the paths became icy.
- I forgot to charge my phone. **Consequently,** I could not record my answer.

### Hence

Formal. Also works before a noun phrase.

- The train was delayed. **Hence,** we arrived late.
- I slept badly—**hence my tired expression**.

### For this reason

- I want to work internationally. **For this reason,** I am improving my English.
- My friend always supports me. **For this reason,** I trust her.

### Due to

**Due to + noun/-ing** introduces a **cause**, not a result. It appears twice in the chart.

- The flight was cancelled **due to bad weather**.
- **Due to a technical problem,** the lesson started late.

## 6. Illustration — Giving Examples and Making Meaning Clear

### For example / For instance

- I enjoy outdoor activities. **For example,** I often go jogging.
- Some traditions bring families together. **For instance,** relatives gather for festival meals.

### Such as

Follow with examples, usually noun phrases.

- I enjoy outdoor activities **such as jogging and hiking**.
- We discussed languages **such as Russian and French**.

### Including

Introduces members of a larger group.

- I packed warm clothes, **including gloves and a scarf**.
- Several people, **including my friend,** offered to help.

### Namely

Identifies exactly what or whom you mean; not just any possible example.

- I want to improve one skill, **namely speaking**.
- Two people joined me, **namely my friend and her partner**.

### In this case

Means “in this particular situation.”

- The noun is plural. **In this case,** we need “were.”
- The plan is still current. **In this case,** the present tense can be appropriate.

### Proof of this

An incomplete phrase that needs a larger structure. Use only when the evidence really supports the claim.

- The payment has been completed. This receipt is **proof of this**.
- She passed the exam; the official certificate provides **proof of this**.

### Like

Can introduce examples in informal speech. **Such as** is often clearer in formal writing.

- I enjoy quiet activities **like reading and drawing**.
- Cities **like Beijing and Shanghai** offer many cultural activities.

### To demonstrate / To clarify

**Demonstrate** shows how something works; **clarify** makes the meaning clearer.

- **To demonstrate how it works,** I'll give you an example.
- **To clarify,** I mean my former teacher, not my current teacher.

## 7. Comparison — Showing Similarities

### Similarly

- My friend enjoys travelling. **Similarly,** I love exploring new places.
- Reading builds vocabulary. **Similarly,** listening introduces useful expressions.

### Equally

As a connector, it presents another point as equally valid or important.

- Grammar matters. **Equally,** clear pronunciation matters.
- The course suits beginners. **Equally,** it can help learners who need revision.

### Likewise

- She supports me when I am worried. **Likewise,** I support her.
- My teacher values preparation. **Likewise,** I try to plan my answers.

### Just as

Often **just as + clause, another clause**.

- **Just as exercise strengthens the body,** practice strengthens speaking skills.
- **Just as my friend listens to me,** I listen to her.

### Just like

Use before a noun or pronoun in these comparisons.

- **Just like my friend,** I enjoy travelling.
- Learning to speak takes practice, **just like learning to swim**.

### Similar to

- This exercise is **similar to** the one we did yesterday.
- My study routine is **similar to** hers.

### Same as

Usually **the same as**, rather than bare **same as**.

- My answer is **the same as** yours.
- This textbook is **the same as** the one my teacher uses.

### By the same token

Extends the same reasoning to another situation; more formal than **also**.

- We expect teachers to prepare. **By the same token,** students should prepare too.
- I want others to respect my privacy. **By the same token,** I should respect theirs.

### In the same way

- I learn vocabulary by repeating examples. **In the same way,** I learn pronunciation through repetition.
- Good teachers support their students. **In the same way,** good managers support their teams.

## 8. Summary — Bringing Your Points Together

### In conclusion

Formal; useful at the end of an essay or presentation.

- **In conclusion,** cultural education helps people understand their heritage.
- **In conclusion,** consistent practice is more useful than occasional long sessions.

### To summarise

- **To summarise,** we need clear ideas, accurate grammar, and regular practice.
- **To summarise,** the trip was challenging but memorable.

### Altogether

Can mean “considering everything” or “in total.” **All in all** is often a clearer everyday summary marker.

- **Altogether,** it was an enjoyable evening.
- We spent two hours studying **altogether**.

### In short

- **In short,** I want to speak clearly and confidently.
- **In short,** fame has both advantages and disadvantages.

### To sum up

- **To sum up,** my friend is someone I can always rely on.
- **To sum up,** the experience taught me to prepare more carefully.

### In summary

- **In summary,** both methods can be useful.
- **In summary,** our traditions can change without losing all their meaning.

### Briefly

Means “in a few words” or “for a short time”; it does not always signal a conclusion.

- **Briefly,** the plan is to read, record, and repeat.
- Let me **briefly** explain why I chose this topic.

### To conclude

- **To conclude,** privacy should be respected even when someone is famous.
- **To conclude,** language learning has made me more curious about other cultures.

## 9. Reason — Explaining Why or What For

### Because of

**Because of + noun/-ing**; **because + clause**.

- I stayed home **because of the rain**.
- I felt more confident **because of my teacher's encouragement**.

### With this in mind

Means “considering what was just mentioned,” rather than directly meaning “because.”

- I only have twenty minutes. **With this in mind,** I'll practise one topic.
- The audience includes beginners. **With this in mind,** I'll use simple examples.

### In fact

Adds a stronger fact or corrects an assumption; it does not directly introduce a reason.

- I thought Russian would be easy. **In fact,** I found the pronunciation challenging.
- My friend is supportive. **In fact,** she was the first person I called.

### In order to

Introduces a **purpose**, using the base verb.

- I read aloud **in order to improve** my pronunciation.
- We left early **in order to avoid** heavy traffic.

### Due to

Same expression as in section 5: introduce the cause, not a full clause.

- The museum was closed **due to renovation work**.
- **Due to a change in my schedule,** I moved my lesson to Sunday.

## 10. Condition — Saying What Depends on What

### If

- **If I have time tonight,** I will read every example.
- **If I were famous,** I would try to protect my family's privacy.

### In that case

Responds to a situation already mentioned.

- “The museum is closed.” “**In that case,** let's visit the park.”
- “You have ten minutes.” “**In that case,** I'll practise one answer.”

### In case

Means “as a precaution because something might happen,” not simply **if**.

- Take a jacket **in case it gets cold**.
- Save your notes **in case your computer crashes**.

### Unless

Usually means **if… not**. Avoid an unnecessary second negative.

- I won't improve **unless I practise**.
- We will go hiking **unless it rains**.

## 11. Concession — Acknowledging a Difficulty or Opposing Point

### Admittedly

Acknowledges a point before adding a limitation or different perspective.

- **Admittedly,** the list is long, but the examples are short.
- **Admittedly,** I still make mistakes, but I can express more ideas now.

### All the same

Means “despite that.”

- I was nervous. **All the same,** I answered the question.
- It was expensive. **All the same,** I enjoyed the meal.

### Up to a point

Means partly or to a limited extent.

- I agree **up to a point**, but privacy still matters.
- Memorising sentences helps **up to a point**, but I also need spontaneous practice.

### Even so

Repeated in the chart; same meaning as in section 3.

- The lesson was challenging. **Even so,** I enjoyed it.
- I had little time. **Even so,** I managed to practise.

### In spite of

Repeated in the chart; follow with a noun or -ing form.

- **In spite of the noise,** I could understand the teacher.
- **In spite of making mistakes,** I kept speaking.

### Although / Even though

Follow with a clause. **Even though** makes the contrast stronger. Avoid **although… but…** in the same construction.

- **Although I felt nervous,** I finished my answer.
- **Even though the weather was terrible,** we enjoyed our holiday.

### Even if

Means “whether or not this happens”; the situation need not be a fact.

- **Even if I make mistakes,** I will keep speaking.
- **Even if it rains tomorrow,** we will still visit the museum.

Compare: **Even though it is raining** = the rain is a fact. **Even if it rains** = consider that possibility.

### However

- I wanted to continue. **However,** it was getting late.
- The answer was accurate. **However,** it needed a clearer example.

## 12. Generalisation — Describing the Usual Situation

### As a rule

- **As a rule,** I review new expressions before bed.
- **As a rule,** short answers are easier to organise.

### For the most part

Means mostly, allowing some exceptions.

- **For the most part,** I understood the lesson.
- The trip was, **for the most part,** enjoyable.

### In general / Generally

- **In general,** I prefer quiet places.
- **Generally,** I remember phrases better when I use them in a story.

### On the whole

Gives an overall judgement, allowing exceptions.

- **On the whole,** I am pleased with my progress.
- **On the whole,** the course has been helpful.

### Overall

Can summarise an evaluation; it is not always a statement about what is usually true.

- **Overall,** the advantages outweigh the disadvantages.
- **Overall,** my second recording was clearer than my first.

### In most cases

- **In most cases,** a simple expression is enough.
- **In most cases,** I can correct the mistake after listening to my recording.

## 13. Short Passages for Repeated Reading

These combine a few useful expressions at a time. Natural speech does not need a connector in every sentence.

### My English Study Plan

> **First,** I read a few examples aloud. **Then,** I record my own sentences. **In addition,** I review phrases from earlier lessons. I still make mistakes. **However,** I am becoming more confident. **For example,** I can now explain my opinions more clearly. **Overall,** this routine is helping me improve.

### A Friend

> My friend and I have a lot in common. **For example,** we both enjoy travelling. **Unlike me,** she is very outgoing. **Even though** our personalities are different, we get on well. She supports me, and **likewise,** I try to support her. **Above all,** we trust each other.

### Learning a Language

> **In general,** learning a language takes patience. Pronunciation can be difficult, **especially** when a sound does not exist in your first language. **Nevertheless,** regular practice helps. I listen carefully **in order to** notice the difference between sounds. **As a result,** my pronunciation gradually improves. **In short,** progress comes from practice.

### Fame and Privacy

> Fame can create opportunities. **On the other hand,** it can reduce privacy. **For instance,** celebrities may be photographed while spending time with their children. **Admittedly,** public figures attract attention, but their families still deserve respect. **For this reason,** I would prefer a quieter life.

### A Winter Trip

> **Before** our trip, we checked the weather. It was going to be extremely cold. **With this in mind,** we packed warm clothes, **including** gloves and scarves. We took extra food **in case** we were delayed. **Despite the cold,** we enjoyed the scenery. **In the end,** everything went well.

### Preserving Culture

> Cultural traditions can create a sense of belonging. **For example,** festivals bring families together. **Moreover,** traditional crafts connect people with local history. **However,** preserving culture does not mean keeping every practice unchanged. **In my opinion,** we should protect valuable traditions while reconsidering harmful practices. **To sum up,** preservation can involve thoughtful change.

**Extra expressions in these passages:** **then** introduces the next step; **in the end** introduces the final outcome; **in my opinion** introduces a personal judgement. They are added for natural storytelling and are not extra entries from the chart.

## 14. Quick Recall Practice

Complete each sentence before checking the answers.

1. I went out ______ the rain. *(despite / although)*
2. I stayed home ______ I was tired. *(because / because of)*
3. Take a charger ______ your battery runs out. *(in case / unless)*
4. My friend is similar ______ me.
5. I was tired. ______, I finished the exercise. *(However / Because of)*
6. ______ I make mistakes tomorrow, I will keep speaking. *(Even if / Despite)*
7. We enjoy activities ______ hiking and swimming. *(such as / namely)*
8. I read aloud in order ______ improve my pronunciation.
9. I won't remember the phrases ______ I review them. *(unless / in case)*
10. I want to improve one skill, ______ speaking. *(namely / including)*

**Answers:** 1. **despite**; 2. **because**; 3. **in case**; 4. **to**; 5. **However**; 6. **Even if**; 7. **such as**; 8. **to**; 9. **unless**; 10. **namely**.
