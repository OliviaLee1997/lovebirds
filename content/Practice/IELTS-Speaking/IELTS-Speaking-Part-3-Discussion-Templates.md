# IELTS Speaking Part 3 — Discussion Templates for Band 7–9 Practice

**Privacy note:** Personal and third-party details have been removed or generalised. First-person stories and altered quotations are anonymised study examples, not a biographical record or verbatim transcript.

**Part 3 is a discussion of broader ideas connected to the Part 2 topic.** It lasts about **4–5 minutes**. The examiner may ask you to explain opinions, compare situations, analyse causes and effects, suggest solutions, or speculate about the future.

**Added study material:** All questions, templates, examples, and exercises below were created for practice. They are not official test questions, predictions, quotations from your teacher, or examiner-scored answers. Examples are illustrative unless explicitly stated otherwise.

These frameworks support **Band 7–9 preparation** by helping you develop clear ideas and use language flexibly. A template does not guarantee a score. IELTS assesses **fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation**, with equal weighting, across the Speaking test.

## Moving from Personal Experience to Broader Discussion

| Part | Example question | Appropriate focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Part 1** | Do you like visiting parks? | Your own preference and a brief reason. |
| **Part 2** | Describe a park you enjoy visiting. | One particular place, with details and personal experience. |
| **Part 3** | Why are public parks important in cities? | Wider benefits, different users, practical considerations, and examples. |

You can use a personal example in Part 3, but explain the broader point it illustrates. “I like parks because they are quiet” can become: **“Parks give residents somewhere to relax, which can be especially valuable in neighbourhoods where people have little private outdoor space.”**

## How to Use the Templates

- Replace **`[brackets]`** with relevant ideas and details.
- Choose **one** expression from each **`A / B / C`** group. Alternatives may carry different meanings, so select what you actually want to say.
- Treat the structures as flexible options. A comparison needs differences; a prediction needs a reason for expecting change.
- Develop one or two points clearly. There is **no prescribed word count, number of sentences, or speaking time for each reply**.
- Allow the examiner to guide the discussion and respond to follow-up questions. You do not have a separate one-minute preparation period for each question.

**A useful default: Position → Explanation → Example or consequence → Qualification, if relevant.**

An example should show **how or why** your point works. A qualification should identify a real limit or condition. You do not need to give both sides of every question or finish every answer with “In conclusion.”

## 1. Opinions and Importance: What Do You Think, and Why?

**Practice questions:** How important are public parks in cities? Do you think creativity is important at work?

**Pattern:** Clear position → Main reason → Broader consequence → Relevant qualification.

### Flexible Answer

> **I'd say that / In my view, / I think** [position]. The main reason is that [explanation]. This means [consequence for a relevant group]. For example, [specific illustration]. **That said, / At the same time,** [a limit or condition, if it affects your view].

**Model — “How important are public parks in cities?”**

> I'd say they're very important, particularly in neighbourhoods where people have little outdoor space at home. They give residents somewhere to walk, exercise or meet without having to buy anything. For example, a small local park can provide both a play area for children and somewhere for older residents to sit. Its value depends partly on how accessible and well maintained it is, though. A park that feels unsafe is less likely to be used.

**Development move:** Explain **who benefits and how**. “It is important for society” is too general unless you develop it.

## 2. Causes: Why Does Something Happen?

**Practice questions:** Why do some people prefer shopping online? Why might people find it difficult to maintain friendships as adults?

**Pattern:** Main cause → Explain the mechanism → Add a distinct cause if useful.

### Flexible Answer

> **One major reason is that / I think a key factor is that** [cause]. When [situation], people may [behaviour], because [explanation]. **Another factor is / A further reason is** [a noun phrase or a that-clause giving a different cause]. This is particularly relevant for [group or context].

**Model — “Why do some people prefer shopping online?”**

> One major reason is that it fits around people's schedules. Someone who works late can order something from home instead of trying to reach a shop before it closes. Another factor is the ease of comparing prices and product details. That can be especially useful for an expensive purchase. Of course, the appeal varies by product: someone may happily buy a familiar item online but prefer to try on shoes in a shop.

**Development move:** Give genuinely different reasons, such as **time, access, cost, trust, or social pressure**. “It's convenient” and “it's easy” may repeat the same point unless you explain the difference.

## 3. Effects: What Difference Does It Make?

**Practice questions:** How can tourism affect a local community? What effects can remote work have on family life?

**Pattern:** Identify an effect → Show the causal chain → Consider a different group or time scale.

### Flexible Answer

> **One possible effect is that / It can mean that** [outcome]. That's because [explanation of the link]. For [group], this may lead to [further consequence]. **However, / At the same time,** [another effect or a limit]. The overall impact depends partly on [relevant condition].

**Model — “How can tourism affect a local community?”**

> Tourism can create demand for local businesses, such as restaurants, guides and small shops. That may provide jobs and help some services remain viable. However, a large number of visitors can also put pressure on transport and public spaces, particularly in a small town. The effects may be uneven: business owners could benefit while residents experience more noise or congestion. The overall impact depends partly on how tourism is managed and whether local people share in the benefits.

**Development move:** Build a chain: **more visitors → more demand for services → business opportunities**, then consider **pressure on transport or housing** where relevant. Explain the connection rather than listing unrelated effects.

## 4. Comparisons: How Are Two Things Different?

**Practice questions:** How does online learning compare with learning in a classroom? How might leisure activities differ between cities and rural areas?

**Pattern:** Choose a dimension → Compare both sides → Give a practical example → Identify who benefits.

### Flexible Answer

> **One important difference is / The main difference I can see is** [dimension]. With [A], people can [feature], whereas with [B], they [contrasting feature]. For example, [illustration using the same dimension]. **Which works better depends on / The more suitable option depends on** [needs or circumstances].

**Model — “How does online learning compare with learning in a classroom?”**

> One important difference is flexibility. A recorded online lesson lets learners study at a convenient time and repeat difficult sections, whereas a classroom course usually follows a fixed schedule. On the other hand, being in the same room can make spontaneous discussion and practical feedback easier. For example, a teacher may notice a student's confusion and respond immediately. Which works better depends on the subject, the course design and how much support the learner needs.

**Development move:** Compare the same dimension, such as **cost, flexibility, interaction, access, or quality**. Avoid broad claims that all young people, older people, or rural residents behave alike.

## 5. Past and Present: How Has Something Changed?

**Practice questions:** How has photography changed people's lives? How has the way people keep in touch changed?

**Pattern:** Earlier situation → Current situation → Main driver → Implication.

### Flexible Answer

> **In the past, / A few decades ago,** [earlier situation]. These days, [present contrast]. A major reason for this change is [driver]. As a result, [consequence]. **However, some things have remained similar / Even so, the change has not been the same everywhere**: [explain the continuity or variation you chose].

**Model — “How has the way people take photographs changed?”**

> In the past, people using film cameras had to think about the cost of each roll and wait to see the results. Today, many people can take and review photos immediately on a phone. That makes photography much more spontaneous: someone can record an ordinary meal or a small moment with friends. It also means people may accumulate so many pictures that they rarely look through them. Even so, photographs still help people preserve memories.

**Development move:** Identify a real contrast and its cause. Use **many people**, **in some places**, or a specific period when a claim is not universal.

## 6. Advantages and Disadvantages: What Are the Trade-offs?

**Practice questions:** What are the advantages and disadvantages of working from home? What are the benefits and drawbacks of living in a large city?

**Pattern:** Main benefit and why → Main drawback and why → Contextual judgement.

### Flexible Answer

> **A clear advantage is that / One benefit is that** [benefit]. This allows [group] to [practical consequence]. **The main drawback is that / One potential downside is that** [drawback], which can [consequence]. On balance, I'd say [judgement], particularly when [relevant condition].

**Model — “What are the advantages and disadvantages of working from home?”**

> A clear advantage is that people can avoid commuting, leaving more time for rest or family responsibilities. It may also be easier to concentrate on individual tasks in a quiet home environment. The main drawback is that informal communication can become harder, especially for a new employee who needs frequent guidance. Home is not always a suitable workspace either. So its value depends on the job, the person's living conditions and how well the team communicates.

**Development move:** Choose one substantial benefit and one substantial drawback. If the question asks only for advantages, answer that first; you do not automatically need a matching list of disadvantages.

## 7. Solutions: What Could Be Done?

**Practice questions:** How could cities reduce traffic congestion? What could schools do to encourage reading?

**Pattern:** Specific action → Who would take it → Why it would help → Limitation or complementary step.

### Flexible Answer

> **One practical step would be to / A useful starting point would be to** [base verb phrase]. [Responsible actor] could [specific action], which would [mechanism or likely result]. **This would be more effective if / It would work best if** [supporting condition]. A further step could be [a complementary action].

**Model — “How could cities reduce traffic congestion?”**

> One practical step would be to make public transport a more reliable alternative to driving. Local authorities could improve bus frequency and give buses priority on busy roads, so journey times become more predictable. This would work best if routes served the places people actually need to reach. Safer walking and cycling routes could also reduce short car journeys. The right combination would depend on the city's layout and the needs of people who cannot easily switch transport.

**Development move:** Replace “The government should raise awareness” with a specific action and a reason it could change behaviour. Awareness may help, but explain what people would learn or do differently.

## 8. Responsibility: Who Should Take the Lead?

**Practice questions:** Who should teach children about managing money: parents or schools? Who should be responsible for keeping public spaces clean?

**Pattern:** Identify the main or shared responsibility → Match roles to capabilities → Explain cooperation.

### Flexible Answer

> **I'd see it as a shared responsibility / I think both groups have a role to play**. [Group A] is well placed to [role], because [reason]. [Group B], meanwhile, can [different role]. If one group had to take the lead, I'd choose [group] for [specific task], while the other could [supporting role].

If your view is that one group should mainly be responsible, begin with **“I think [group] should take the lead because…”** and develop that position. Do not force a shared-responsibility answer.

**Model — “Who should teach children about managing money: parents or schools?”**

> I think both have a role to play. Parents can involve children in everyday decisions, such as comparing prices or saving for something they want. Schools can provide a basic foundation so that children have a chance to learn even if money is rarely discussed at home. For example, a lesson could show how a simple budget works, while parents could help children apply it to pocket money. The two approaches could reinforce each other.

**Development move:** Explain each group's distinct role. “Everyone is responsible” is a starting point, not a developed answer.

## 9. Predictions: What Might Happen in the Future?

**Practice questions:** Do you think printed books will disappear? How might technology change the way people learn?

**Pattern:** Qualified prediction → Reason → Likely area of change → Uncertainty or exception.

### Flexible Answer

> **I think it's likely that / I would expect that** [prediction]. One reason is that [current factor supporting it]. We may see [specific development], especially in [context]. **However, it's less clear whether / What is harder to predict is whether** [uncertain outcome]. Much will depend on [condition].

**Model — “Do you think printed books will disappear?”**

> I doubt they'll disappear completely, because they serve purposes beyond simply delivering text. Some readers enjoy the physical experience, and books can also be gifts or objects people want to keep. Digital reading is likely to remain attractive when portability or quick access matters. So I would expect both formats to continue, with their popularity varying by purpose. The balance may change, but it is difficult to predict how quickly people's preferences will shift.

**Development move:** Support the prediction with a reason. **Likely / may / could / I would expect** express different degrees of confidence; they do not make an unsupported claim convincing by themselves.

## 10. Conditions and Exceptions: Is It Always True?

**Practice questions:** Does technology always make life easier? Is travelling abroad necessary to understand other cultures?

**Pattern:** Direct qualified answer → Name the deciding factor → Compare situations → State your view.

### Flexible Answer

> **Not necessarily / Not in every situation**. It depends mainly on [specific factor]. If [condition A], then [outcome A]. However, when [condition B], [outcome B]. So I'd say [your position with a clear boundary].

**Model — “Does technology always make life easier?”**

> Not necessarily. It depends on how well the technology suits the task and the person using it. A clear booking app can save time, but a confusing system may make a simple task much harder, particularly if there is no alternative way to get help. Technology can also create extra work through constant notifications or repeated updates. So I would judge it by whether it removes a real difficulty for users, rather than by how many features it offers.

**Development move:** Follow **“It depends”** with **what it depends on**. Then show how the answer changes under different conditions.

## Expressions for Developing a Discussion

| Purpose | Alternatives |
| --- | --- |
| State a view | **I'd say that… / In my view,… / I tend to think that…** |
| Give the main reason | **A key reason is that… / This is largely because… / One explanation is that…** |
| Explain a mechanism | **This allows people to… / This makes it easier for people to… / This can reduce the need to…** |
| Give an example | **For example,… / A good illustration would be… / Take the case of…** |
| Compare | **Whereas… / By comparison,… / One important difference is…** |
| Add a different consideration | **Another factor is… / There is also the question of… / A further consideration is…** |
| Qualify a claim | **In many cases,… / This is particularly true when… / That may be less relevant for…** |
| Acknowledge a drawback | **One limitation is that… / The difficulty is that… / A possible downside is that…** |
| Express probability | **It seems likely that… / There is a possibility that… / I would expect…** |
| Identify a condition | **Much depends on… / This would work best if… / The outcome may vary according to…** |
| Refine your wording | **More precisely,… / What I mean is… / Perhaps a better way to put it is…** |
| Give a brief judgement | **On balance, I'd say… / For that reason, I think… / In that situation, I would favour…** |

**Keep the grammar with the expression:** **Whereas + clause**; **because + clause**; **because of + noun phrase**; **allows someone to + base verb**; **may + base verb**; **depends on + noun phrase or question clause**.

Examples: “It depends on **the cost**.” / “It depends on **whether people can afford it**.” / “This may **reduce** waiting times.”

## Common Topic Areas and Ideas to Develop

These are practice themes, not a current question bank. The idea paths are possible starting points; choose and defend your own view.

| Theme | Practice questions | Possible idea path |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Public places and the environment** | Why do cities need green spaces? How can people be encouraged to use public parks? | Access to outdoor space → opportunities to walk and meet → importance of safe paths, seating and maintenance. |
| **Technology and work** | Which tasks are suitable for automation? How might technology change teamwork? | Repetitive tasks → possible time savings → need to check errors and preserve useful human judgement. |
| **Education and skills** | Should schools teach practical skills? What makes a good teacher? | Clear explanation and feedback → better understanding → opportunities to apply learning independently. |
| **Friends, family and community** | Why can adult friendships be difficult to maintain? How can communities help people feel connected? | Competing schedules or distance → fewer spontaneous meetings → regular shared activities and accessible meeting places. |
| **Media and public figures** | Why do people follow celebrities? Should public figures be role models? | Entertainment or identification → possible influence → distinguish professional achievement from expertise in unrelated areas. |
| **Travel and culture** | What can people learn from travel? Can tourism harm local traditions? | Contact with different routines → opportunities to question assumptions → depth of understanding depends on meaningful interaction. |
| **Shopping and possessions** | Why do people keep old objects? How can people be encouraged to repair things? | Emotional attachment or usefulness → reasons to keep an item → repairs depend partly on cost, parts and available services. |
| **Photography and memory** | Why do people take photographs? Does photographing an event affect how people experience it? | Recording details or sharing moments → potential memory aid → frequent interruptions may distract from the event itself. |

## Connected Discussion Example: From a Peaceful Place to Public Spaces

Imagine your Part 2 topic was **a peaceful place you enjoy visiting**, such as the wetland park. The following practice discussion shows how to move from one place to broader questions. These are illustrative questions, not a prediction of what an examiner will ask.

### Question 1: Why Do People Need Quiet Places in Cities?

> I think quiet places give people a break from constant stimulation. In a busy city, residents may spend much of the day dealing with traffic, work or crowded transport, so somewhere calmer can help them unwind. That does not have to mean complete silence. A park with natural sounds, for instance, can feel restful even when other people are nearby. What matters is that people have some choice about the pace and intensity of their surroundings.

**Possible follow-up:** “Do you think everyone finds the same places relaxing?”

> Probably not. Some people enjoy a lively café because they find the background activity comforting, while others need much less noise. Familiarity and a sense of safety may matter as much as the actual sound level.

### Question 2: Should Cities Spend More Money on Parks?

> In areas where residents have little access to outdoor space, I think there is a strong case for it. A park can serve several needs at once, from exercise to social contact. However, the decision should reflect local priorities and the cost of looking after the space over time. Improving a neglected existing park may sometimes be more useful than building an impressive new one that is difficult for residents to reach.

**Possible follow-up:** “What would you prioritise with a limited budget?”

> I would start with basic access and maintenance: safe paths, working lights where appropriate, seating and clean facilities. Those improvements could make a park usable for more people before money is spent on decorative features.

### Question 3: How Might Urban Parks Change in the Future?

> I would expect some parks to be designed for a wider range of uses, especially where space is limited. They might combine shaded areas, accessible walking routes and spaces that can absorb rainwater. Local conditions will matter, though. A hot, dry city may have different priorities from one with frequent heavy rain. I also think planners will need to balance popular activities with quieter areas, so one group does not dominate the whole space.

**Notice the progression:** The answers move from **why a space matters**, to **how resources should be allocated**, to **what might change**. Each answer responds to the new question instead of retelling the Part 2 story.

## Making an Answer More Precise

| General statement | More developed version |
| --- | --- |
| “Technology is convenient.” | “Online appointments can save people a journey, particularly when a brief conversation is enough. Their usefulness depends on whether users have suitable access and support.” |
| “The government should do something.” | “Local authorities could improve bus frequency on routes used by commuters, making public transport a more practical option for those journeys.” |
| “Young people like the internet.” | “People who rely on online services for study or work may value quick access to information, although habits vary within every age group.” |
| “Tourism is good for the economy.” | “Visitor spending can support local restaurants and accommodation, but the benefit to residents depends partly on who owns those businesses and who gets the jobs.” |

**Use examples honestly:** A hypothetical example can start **“For example, a parent who works late might…”** You do not need invented surveys or exact statistics to explain a reasonable point.

**When a topic is unfamiliar:** Briefly acknowledge the limit, then address what you can reason about: **“I haven't considered that in detail, but one possible effect is…”** If you have not understood the question, ask for clarification: **“Could you explain what you mean by…?”**

## Grammar for Clear Reasoning

- **Cause:** “People may drive because bus services are infrequent.”
- **Result:** “This makes it harder for residents to travel without a car.”
- **Comparison:** “Online courses can offer flexibility, whereas classroom lessons provide a shared schedule.”
- **Concession:** “Although a new park could be useful, maintaining existing spaces may be a better first step.”
- **A possible future condition:** “If public transport becomes more reliable, some commuters may choose to use it.”
- **A hypothetical policy:** “If buses were more frequent, more people might consider using them.”
- **A cautious generalisation:** “People with limited storage space may prefer digital copies.”

Choose grammar that expresses the relationship you mean. Complexity is useful when it improves the explanation and remains accurate.

## Review Practice

### A. Recognise the Question Type

Match each question to a discussion pattern:

1. Why do some people keep things they no longer use?
2. How is studying alone different from studying in a group?
3. What could encourage people to use public transport?
4. Will physical shops become less common?
5. Does more choice always make consumers happier?

**Answers:** 1. Causes. 2. Comparison. 3. Solutions. 4. Prediction. 5. Conditions and exceptions.

### B. Build an Explanation

Choose one statement and add **why → how → an example → a relevant limit**. These are planning prompts; you do not have to use all four in every answer.

- Parks can benefit a community.
- Online learning can be useful.
- Practical skills should be taught at school.

**Example development:**

> Online learning can be useful because it removes the need to travel to a classroom. That may make a course accessible to someone who lives far away or has caring responsibilities. For example, a parent could watch a recorded lesson after their child is asleep. However, they would still need suitable internet access and a way to ask questions.

### C. Respond to a Challenge

After giving an opinion, practise one follow-up:

- **Would that work for everyone?**
- **What might be a disadvantage?**
- **Who would pay for it?**
- **Has that always been true?**

Start by answering the follow-up itself. You may refine your position: **“That would be harder for… / In that situation, I would… / You're right that cost matters; one option would be…”** Use the last option only if you actually agree with the point raised.

### D. Correct the Reasoning Language

1. This allows people save time.
2. It depends of whether people can afford it.
3. Although it is convenient, but it can be expensive.
4. If public transport will improve, more people may use it.
5. Young people always prefer online learning.

**Suggested answers:**

1. **This allows people to save time.**
2. **It depends on whether people can afford it.**
3. **Although it is convenient, it can be expensive.** Alternatively: **It is convenient, but it can be expensive.**
4. **If public transport improves, more people may use it.**
5. The grammar is correct, but the claim is too absolute without support. A more defensible version is: **Some young people may prefer online learning because it offers flexibility.** Explain the relevant circumstances.

### Self-Check

1. Did I answer the exact question and make my position clear?
2. Did I explain the connection between my claim and its consequence?
3. Did my example support the point rather than change the subject?
4. Did I identify relevant conditions without becoming vague?
5. Could I express the same idea again with different words?
6. Was my speech easy to follow, with clear pronunciation and pauses between ideas?
