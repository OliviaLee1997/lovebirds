# Cue Card 08 — A Language You Learned: Russian

**Study note:** Added model answer for practice towards IELTS Speaking Bands 7–9, not an examiner-scored response. First-person stories and altered quotations are anonymised study examples. Grammar notes retain qualifications and transcription uncertainty.

## Practice Cue Card

Describe a foreign language you have learned. Say when you learned it, how you studied it, what you found difficult, and what the experience taught you.

## Extended Model Answer

One foreign language I studied was Russian. I started learning it at university, where I had opportunities to learn from Russian speakers. Before describing it, I should make clear that studying a language and becoming completely comfortable using it are different things. My experience was valuable, but I wouldn't want to imply that I can now discuss any subject fluently in Russian. What I remember most is the challenge of producing unfamiliar sounds and the way the course introduced me to something beyond the words on a vocabulary list.

Our learning involved textbooks, tests and poems. **At first**, it is easy to think of those as separate parts of a course: you learn the material, prepare for a test and then move on. **However**, poetry brings in another dimension. Even when you cannot understand every word immediately, you start to notice rhythm and the relationship between sound and meaning. For me, that is one reason a language feels different when you hear it used in a complete text rather than only in short exercises designed to practise a particular rule.

Pronunciation was one of the hardest parts, especially the trilled Russian r. It is a consonant produced with a vibrating tongue tip, and knowing that description doesn't mean you can produce it straight away. **In other words**, understanding the explanation and controlling the movement are two different skills. That difficulty helped me appreciate why pronunciation needs patient practice. If I were working on it again, I would try short, focused sessions with reliable feedback instead of repeating the sound rapidly and hoping that more effort alone would solve the problem.

**Another valuable aspect** was the opportunity to hear Russian speakers. A textbook can explain a structure, but a person gives you an example of how the language sounds as communication. You notice where the speaker pauses and which words seem to carry the important information. **At the same time**, hearing a fluent speaker can make your own limitations very obvious. I think it helps to see that gap as a guide to what to practise, rather than as evidence that you are somehow unsuited to learning languages.

**Looking back**, the experience changed how I think about language study. I still value grammar and vocabulary, because they give you something to work with, but I also see the importance of actually using what you know. That is relevant to my English learning now. I want to be able to express an idea, listen to a response and continue the conversation. **Ultimately**, learning Russian reminded me that progress involves several abilities developing together. Passing a test can be encouraging, but the more satisfying goal is being able to connect sounds and structures with something you really want to say.

## Linking Language

**At first** introduces an initial perspective; **however / at the same time** qualify it; **in other words** reformulates; **another valuable aspect** adds; **looking back / ultimately** introduce reflection.

## Your Grammar Review

| Reported wording | Correction | Pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “I started a learning Russian.” | I **started learning Russian**. | No article before this verb complement; **started to learn** also works. |
| “Three foreigner languages” | three **foreign languages** | **Foreign** is the adjective; **foreigner** is a person. |
| “a vowel… that is not exist” | a sound that **does not exist** in my first language | **Exist** is the main verb: use **does not exist**, not **is not exist**. |
| “a lot of poem” | a lot of **poems** | Plural countable noun; compare uncountable **poetry**. |

**Accuracy point:** the trilled r is a consonant, not a vowel.

## Shorten for the Exam

Keep university, the learning methods, the trilled-r difficulty, and one lesson for your current English study. Remove the longer reflections on poetry and testing.
