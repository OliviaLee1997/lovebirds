# Cue Card 20 — A Book You Are Reading: Politics and National Development

**Study note:** Added model answer for practice towards IELTS Speaking Bands 7–9, not an examiner-scored response. First-person stories and altered quotations are anonymised study examples. Grammar notes retain qualifications and transcription uncertainty.

**Additional practice topic:** another way to develop your existing experiences and interests.

## Practice Cue Card

Describe a book you are reading or have read. Say what it is about, why you chose it, what interests you, and how you feel about it.

## Extended Model Answer

I'd like to talk about a book about politics and national development that I've been reading. It discusses why socialism is considered suitable for China and how countries organise their resources and development. What interests me is the connection between very large decisions and the different circumstances of particular regions. It is easy to use a phrase such as national development as though it refers to one simple process. I find it more interesting to ask what that process involves and why the same approach may have different effects in different places.

**One question that attracts my attention is** how the size of a country changes the problems it has to solve. A very large country may contain regions with quite different resources and priorities. A smaller country faces its own constraints, but the scale of coordination is different. **For example**, a decision about where to invest in infrastructure can raise questions about economic opportunity, access and the relationship between regions. I don't expect a single book to settle every question, but I like reading material that helps me identify what the questions actually are.

Technology is another part of the subject that interests me because I work in a technical field. I often think about technology at the level of a product or a particular task. **By contrast**, reading about national development encourages me to consider a wider setting: who can use a technology, what resources it requires and how its benefits might be distributed. **At the same time**, I try to distinguish between what the author argues and what has been established by evidence. Understanding an argument doesn't require agreeing with every part of it immediately.

For this kind of reading, I generally prefer a paper book. I find it comfortable for a longer session, and I like being able to write notes in the margins. **When an idea needs more thought**, I can pause and work through it without feeling that I should immediately move to another piece of information. E-books are useful too, especially for searching or carrying several titles. **For me**, though, the physical format suits the kind of slow attention I want to give a complicated subject, particularly when I am reading at home.

**What I value most is** the opportunity to move beyond a quick opinion. Political and economic discussions can become very compressed, with people using familiar labels instead of explaining the reasoning behind them. Reading a longer argument gives me a chance to examine the steps and notice where I still have questions. **Ultimately**, I would describe this as a book that encourages curiosity rather than one that has given me a final answer to everything. That makes the experience worthwhile: I finish a reading session with more to think about, not simply another slogan to repeat.

## Linking Language

**For example** illustrates; **by contrast / at the same time** compare and qualify; **when** introduces a condition in a routine; **for me** marks personal preference; **ultimately** gives the final evaluation.
